# Candidate phrasings for the early question, ranked

Judge: judge-010, 2026-10-04. Rules every candidate meets: one question; asks for facts and years,
not feelings or reasons; words a person on the street answers; no theory word; no exclamation point;
no list of answers to pick from (the coach never offers one, agent_fidelity.md "Closing a reply");
and never the word "period", which the coach is told never to say (coach_story_shape.md), so the
candidates say "times" and "years". The coach asks in its own words; these are the shapes.
Sources: FE = Family Evaluation; FTiCP = Family Therapy in Clinical Practice; BS = Basic Series.
All answers below are fictional.

## 1. "Looking back over your life so far, what were the two or three times when the most was going on, and about what years were they?"

Theory reason. "The most going on" is Kerr's own test of a period, "a series of stressful events
converged" (FE ch10 L45), and his confluence of several changes in a short span (FE ch9 L68), in
plain words. It asks about events, not feelings, so it fits "what happened, and how, when, and
where" and avoids "why" (FTiCP ch16 L99; Gilbert L785-792). It does not lead toward bad years: a
wedding, a birth, a move or a return disturb the balance as a loss does (FTiCP ch15 L13; BS 6
L88-105), and this wording lets the person name them. Asking the years in the same question makes
the answer dated from the first reply (FE ch10 L11, "fairly exact dates"). "Two or three" asks for a count, not a list of
answers, and keeps the follow-up to a few turns.
Example answer. "Probably 2009 to 2011, when Dad was in and out of hospital and I quit school. And
2019, the year Marcus and I split and I moved back to Duluth."

## 2. "When you look back, which times in your life stand out the most, and roughly what years were they?"

Theory reason. The brief's own shape. "Stand out" asks for the person's own markers with no bias
toward good or bad, so it catches additions and losses alike (FTiCP ch15 L13). Its weakness is the
theory's: what stands out to a person is the symptom and the charged issue, while the related
events are what the family "operates always to obscure and misremember" (FTiCP ch9 L93; BS 6
L120-123), so the answer leans harder toward the person's own symptom years than candidate 1, and
it may draw single days ("my wedding day") rather than times. Still a clean early question, with
the years asked in the same breath.
Example answer. "The year I was fourteen, 1999, when we moved to Spokane. And 2015, after my son
was born."

## 3. "Which times in your life were the most stressful for your family, and about when were they?"

Theory reason. Kerr's catch-all, "Have there been other periods of major stress on the family?"
(FE ch10 L15), moved to the front and put in common words. Its strength is lineage and that it asks
about the family, not only the person, which pulls toward the wider family Kerr surveys on both
sides (FE ch10 L19). Its
weaknesses: "stressful" asks for a feeling about the years, and felt stress is not stress (FE ch10
L65, note 24); it leads toward bad years, so additions go unnamed; and the person may not know what
was stressful for the family, only for themselves (FE ch10 L7).
Example answer. "The year Grandpa Ike died, I think 2004. And when my parents nearly divorced, that
was 2012 or so."

## 4. "What were the two or three biggest things that happened in your family while you were growing up, and about what year was each?"

Theory reason. Asks for events straight out, the way the theory finds periods: someone added,
someone lost, a move (FTiCP ch15 L13; FTiCP ch9 L89). Yields dated nodal events rather than
labelled years, so the record gets events at once. Weaknesses: "while you were growing up" cuts
off adult life, where what brings an adult to coaching usually sits (the judge's inference, not a
source), and "biggest" is a ranking
the person may answer with the charged issue. Best as the follow-up for a person who cannot name
times, or as the shape for the years before the person's memory ("what happened in your family
before you were five").
Example answer. "My grandmother came to live with us when I was about seven, so 1992. My brother
left for the army in 1998. We moved twice before I was ten."

## 5. "Were there a few years when a lot changed at once for you or your family, and when were they?"

Theory reason. The same target as candidate 1, "a lot changed at once", with "for you or your
family" opening the wider household. Weaker because it opens with a yes-or-no, which a person can
answer "not really" and close, and because "changed" draws moves and jobs more than illness.
Example answer. "Yes, 2016. Mom retired, my sister got divorced, and we sold the farm, all within
about a year."

## 6. "Looking back, when were the hardest years for you, and when were things at their best?"

Theory reason. Two questions in one, so it breaks the one-question rule and would be kept as two
(open_questions.md). Its merit: the best years are where Bowen reads from, the stable years after
the last child (FTiCP ch21 L95), and a symptom that lifted is as telling as one that started (FE
ch10 L15; FE ch9 L114). Its fault: "hardest" and "best" ask for feelings about the years, not
what happened in them, and a person's sense of how hard a time was tracks their reactions, not the
stress (FE ch10 L65, note 24). Use its second half later, as its own question.
Example answer. "Hardest, 2009 for sure. Best, probably the first few years after we married, 2003
to 2006."

## 7. "What years were the big changes in your family: someone born, someone died, a move, a split?"

Theory reason. The examples are the theory's own nodal kinds (FTiCP ch15 L13; FTiCP ch9 L89), and
they tell the person what counts, which helps the one who answers "nothing much happened". But the
examples are a list of places to look, which the coach is told never to hold out (agent_fidelity.md
"Closing a reply"), and they stop the person naming the kind the coach did not list (an illness, a
job lost, a grandparent moving in). Ranked last for that rule.
Example answer. "Born: my sister in 1990. Died: Grandpa in 2004. Moved: 1999. Split: my parents in
2012."

## The judge's call

Candidate 1 first. It asks for what the theory counts as a period (many things at once), in words
anyone answers, with the year in the same breath, and it does not steer toward bad years. Candidate
2 second, as the brief's shape with a known lean toward the person's own symptom years. Candidate 3
third, for lineage. Candidates 4 and 6 (second half) are follow-ups, not the early question. One fork
for the reviewer: whether the years are asked in the same question ("and about what years") or in
the next turn; the judge asks them in the same question because an undated marker is of little use
(FTiCP ch9 L93) and the year is the first thing the follow-up would ask anyway (decide.md, item 6).
