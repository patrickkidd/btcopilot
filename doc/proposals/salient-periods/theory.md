# The early "which periods of your life stand out" question: what the theory says it should get at, and its risks

Judge: judge-010, 2026-10-04. Proposal for the public app repository (fictional people only; no private text).
Sources are cited by book, chapter and line of the corpus text files: FE = Kerr and Bowen, *Family Evaluation*;
FTiCP = Bowen, *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice*; BS = Bowen, *Basic Series* tapes; Gilbert = Gilbert 2006,
*The Eight Concepts of Bowen Theory*. Every line cited was opened by the judge. Where the sources are silent,
the text says so and marks the judge's inference as such.

## 1. The premise, tested against the sources

The brief's premise: people remember what was emotionally loud, so the times they name are the
most intense times of family emotional process, and asking for them early gives quick coverage.

What the sources do. Neither Kerr nor Bowen asks the person to name or rank the times of their
life. Kerr's interview starts with the presenting problem and "fairly exact dates" of when it began
and recurred (FE ch10 L11), then goes through the nuclear family's history from the day the couple
met, each birth, move, separation and job change, asking at each one how each person adapted and whether
anyone had a problem then (FE ch10 L15), then surveys both extended families (FE ch10 L19). Bowen
does the same: "a chronological review of the symptom development ... with specific dates and
circumstances at the time of each symptom eruption" (FTiCP ch9 L85), a "fix" on the whole
household at each nodal event (FTiCP ch9 L89), and the symptom followed "on a calendar year by
year" (BS 3 L481-483). In both, the person supplies dated facts and the clinician derives the
periods. The one open sweep in either source comes after that dated history, as a catch-all: "Have there been
other periods of major stress on the family? What were the circumstances?" (FE ch10 L15).

So the sources do not support the question as a way to find the periods. They support it as the
sweep brought forward: a fast way to get the person's own first markers, each of which is then
pinned to a year, to who was in the house, and to what else happened on both sides of the family
around it. Kerr's order is loose, "it is just necessary to collect all of it eventually" (FE ch10
L65, note 14), so bringing the sweep forward breaks no rule of his; it only must not replace the
dated history. That is the brief's own caveat, and the sources back it.

## 2. What the question should get at

Kerr names two kinds of period worth assessing: "when a series of stressful events converged
and/or when symptoms were prominent", and an impression from several such periods "is more
reliable than an impression formed from just the recent period" (FE ch10 L45). The theory's
periods open when the household's balance is disturbed by someone added or someone lost (FTiCP
ch15 L13; BS 6 L88-105), with the death or threatened death of someone the family leaned on as the
largest disturbance (BS 6 L103-105, L145-153; FE ch9 L74), and they run on for months to years
after a loss in other people's symptoms (FTiCP ch15 L15; FE ch10 L19; BS 6 L112-116). A symptom's
own course is also a period: onset, worse, better, and what moved at each turn (FE ch9 L104-116;
FE ch10 L15 on a symptom that disappears when another appears), and a long-standing condition lit
up or eased as anxiety rises and falls (BS 5 L77-84, L119-121).

A good early question therefore asks for times when a lot was happening, not for times that felt
bad: additions (a marriage, a birth, a return) disturb the balance as losses do (FTiCP ch15 L13),
and a person asked for the "worst" years will not name their wedding year or the year a child was
born. It asks for facts and years, not feelings or reasons: "who, what, where, when, how", never
why (Gilbert L785-792; FTiCP ch16 L99), and it must not be "an opinion disguised as a question"
(FE ch10 L11). What it yields is a short list of markers: the person's own, in their own words,
with a rough year each. Judge's inference: that list will be weighted toward the person's own
symptom periods and the periods of the household they lived in, because those are what a person
can see from where they stood.

## 3. The risks, from the sources

**Risk 1: the list is a list of symptom periods.** What the family offers unasked is the symptom and
the charged issue; the connected events are what it "operates always to obscure and misremember"
(FTiCP ch9 L93). Bowen's shoplifting family could hardly be brought to mention the grandmother's
operation on the same day (BS 6 L120-123); Kerr's families leave out a cancer surgery two years
back unless asked (FE ch10 L15); a father does not volunteer years of heavy drinking until asked
about how he manages anxiety (FE ch10 L37). Felt stress is not stress (FE ch10 L65, note 24). So
the list will tend to hold the years the person's own trouble was up, and to leave out the deaths,
moves and illnesses on the other side of the family that the theory says set them off.

**Risk 2: quiet years are not empty years, and the loudest years are the least readable.** The
family of the 18-year-old reported "few problems or symptoms" before his collapse; Kerr reads the
quiet as anxiety bound in the parents' overinvolvement, not as adaptiveness (FE ch10 L45). The
deep reaction to a death "is greatest when there's little emotional expression at the time" (BS 6
L77-78), so the year the person felt least may carry the most. Bowen reads the family's functioning
from the stable years after the last child was born (FTiCP ch21 L95), and Kerr says triangles are
unreadable both in unusually quiet and in unusually chaotic periods, and "most easily observed
during periods of moderate tension" (FE ch6 L11). A question that collects only the loud years
misses the period Bowen reads from and collects the periods Kerr finds hardest to read.

**Risk 3: the years come out wrong and drift toward the comfortable story.** Within weeks a mother
insisted her daughter's illness came before her own mother's death, when it came ten days after
(FTiCP ch9 L93). "The time I began to stand up to him" stood in for a disturbance in the extended
family (FTiCP ch9 L87). Ages arrive as "eleven or twelve" and "the fifth grade" and need
"persistent questioning and mathematical computation" (FTiCP ch9 L93; BS 3 L488-490). Any one
informant's account "is distorted information ... from memory" (BS 3 L510-511). Since nearness in
time is the only evidence the theory claims, and it is read as suggestion never cause (FE ch10 L11;
FTiCP ch9 L93; BS 6 L119), a time named without a year, or with the order wrong, is of little use.

**Risk 4: the person's own vantage.** The person was too young for some of it: the youngest "cannot
remember a time when there was not someone older" (Gilbert L829), and Bowen names as a nodal point
his grandfather's death when he himself was about four, and lays out the whole household of that
year (FTiCP ch21 L91-93); that a four-year-old's own memory did not supply that household is the
judge's inference, not Bowen's words. They were
too far for some of it: a daughter "a thousand miles away" showed no reaction to her mother's
death, and within six months her own daughter did (BS 6 L115). And a child's memory of a year holds
the bed-wetting, not the aunt's divorce the mother was reacting to (FE ch9 L74). So the times a
person names are times in their own life; the theory's periods are in the family's life, including
years before they were born and households they never lived in. "Almost everyone is unaware of
some important facts about his family" (FE ch10 L7).

**Risk 5: showing a link early loses the person.** Bowen: "I have never been able to use the related
events early in psychotherapy ... Some families found reason to never return" (FTiCP ch9 L93); a
premature connection between a mother's stroke and an affair ended a therapy at six sessions
(FTiCP ch15 L19); "I never tell them about it until well in family psychotherapy" (BS 6 L125).
Kerr is gentler: he asks about events rather than interpreting them, so that "associations may
suddenly dawn on people" (FE ch10 L11), while his notes say this "does not imply that a therapist
never expresses an opinion" (FE ch10 L65). A list of named times will put two things close in time
in front of the coach on the first day; what it makes of that stays in its notes until the dates
are firm.

**Risk 6 (judge's inference, not in the sources):** a question that asks for several times at once
invites several in one answer. Each needs its own year, its own household, its own events; taken
together in one reply they get none. The coach must take them one at a time, and must not write a
shift or a noted event from a label ("2014 to 2016 was bad") before the person says what happened.
A cluster from a label is already impossible: the code computes clusters from dated events and
refuses any group under three at the write (doc/CLUSTERS.md).

## 4. Where the sources are silent

- No source asks the person which times stand out, which were worst, or which were turning points.
  The nearest is Kerr's catch-all after the dated history (FE ch10 L15). The word "telescoping" is nowhere;
  the sources describe reversed order, vague ages and school-grade anchors (FTiCP ch9 L85, L93).
- No source says intense times are remembered better. The sources say the related events are
  forgotten and the symptom is kept in front (section 3, risk 1): a claim about what is left out,
  not about what is kept. The brief's premise is plausible and is marked here as inference.
- No source addresses one person telling their history alone, by text, with no second informant.
  The remedies both authors use for one memory are the spouse, the records and the extended family
  (FE ch10 L15, L23; FTiCP ch9 L83, L93). The app has only the person and the calendar.
- "Recent" and "early" have no numbers. The shock wave runs "several months to more than a year"
  (FE ch10 L19), "months or years" (FTiCP ch15 L15), "about 18 months" (BS 6 L116).

## 5. What follows for the app, in one paragraph

Ask it once, early, as the person's own first markers: times when a lot was happening, in which
years. Treat the answer as where to start, never as the periods. For each time named: the year by
the person's own anchors, who was in the house, what happened to each of them and on both sides of
the family, how each managed; one question per turn, the coach's question following the last
answer. Then go where the person did not point: the years between the named times, the years before
they could remember, the generation above, the relatives "not involved" (FE ch10 L19). Keep Kerr's
catch-all for the end of the dated history. Keep what lines up in the coach's notes until the dates
are firm. The question opens the history; the dated facts, gathered in any order but completely, are
the history.
