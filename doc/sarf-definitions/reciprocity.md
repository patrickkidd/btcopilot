# Over- and underfunctioning: reciprocal functioning (RF)

For coders: what the original authors wrote about overfunctioning and underfunctioning, where to find it, and what is decided. Read it for food for thought while coding. It is not a rule book; section 1 says which rules exist.

- Built 2026-09-26 from the sources listed in [`README.md`](README.md). Every quote below was checked word for word against its source file by the quote checker; the check is repeatable.
- This is the public edition: excerpts from confidential sources are left out, and so are the paths to the source files ([`README.md`](README.md)).
- Ids (RF1, RF2, ...) are stable. Location keys (FE2 L216, IRR M5 @00:54:14, ...) are explained in [`README.md`](README.md).
- Transcripts of recordings are machine transcriptions. A word marked [sic] is as transcribed.
- Bowen's, Kerr's and Havstad's statements are ideas. None was tested with counted data unless the note says so.
- One page, not two. No source describes one side without the other: Bowen calls it "a reciprocating mechanism" (RF2), Kerr says each shapes the other "as much as" the reverse (RF7), and Patrick's value space lists one kind, "reciprocal functioning" (RF14). The old review's two files indexed the same passages twice.

## Coder summary

There is no coding rule for over- or underfunctioning. Patrick rescinded every one on 2026-09-25 (status item 1); the drawing symbol, an up arrow and a down arrow, stands as a drawing rule only (status item 2). Whether overfunctioning is a functioning shift up, down, or not a functioning code at all is undecided (status item 5), and so is which side Kerr's "adaptive one" is (status item 6). Where overfunctioning ends and meeting a real need begins is open for the IRR group (status items 4, 9).

The passages that matter most:
- RF7: each side shapes the other as much as the reverse; there is no villain and no rescuer.
- RF10: Kerr defines both sides against the person's own basic level, not against the partner.
- RF17: Bowen reads the positions from the long-term posture of each to the other, "rather than from verbal reports".
- RF29 and RF32: a flexible version, covering for someone temporarily ill, is described as smooth working, not as a problem.
- RF70 to RF72: the IRR coders' working line: helping an ageing parent down the stairs is not overfunctioning; a mechanism is automatic where a choice exists.

The main off-theory trap: reading the overfunctioner as the strong, healthy one, or as the villain who "enables". Both sides seek support "with about the same intensity" (RF23), and the adaptive one can be on either side (RF44, RF45). A second trap: taking one person's complaint ("I'm doing everything") as the fact of the pattern. Both sides complain in mirror terms (RF18), and the old review's "complaints about others" marker is not in Kerr (RF79, RF80).

## 2. What the original authors wrote

### 2a. What it is: one process, two positions

#### Bowen

- <a id="RF98"></a>**RF98** “each family member functions in a variety of ways, determined quite as much by the reciprocal functioning of other family members as by forces inside himself.” Bowen, FTiCP ch2 L5. The early reason for coding functioning shifts in pairs rather than as traits.
- <a id="RF4"></a>**RF4** “Either mother or father can function in either position though they eventually find an equilibrium in which one is overadequate in most areas and the other overadequate in fewer areas.” Bowen, FTiCP ch3 L17. Positions can be held area by area.
- <a id="RF3"></a>**RF3** “The terms overadequate and inadequate refer to functioning states and not fixed states. Overadequate refers to a functioning facade of strength that is greater than realistic.” Bowen, FTiCP ch4 L54. A state that can change, not a trait of the person.
- <a id="RF1"></a>**RF1** “One of the most important aspects of family dysfunction is an equal degree of overfunction in another part of the family system. It is factual that dysfunctioning and overfunctioning exist together.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35. Bowen names the pair, never one side alone.
- <a id="RF2"></a>**RF2** “However it develops, the overfunction-dysfunction is a reciprocating mechanism. In previous papers (Bowen 1960), I called this the "overadequate-inadequate reciprocity."” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35. The older name for the same process.
- <a id="RF5"></a>**RF5** “The more one spouse under-functions, the more the other sort of over-functions or makes up for the dysfunction.” Bowen, BS5 L186.
- <a id="RF6"></a>**RF6** “And she functions poorly in reciprocal functioning to the husband who functions better. So having the symptom for these two makes it better.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:06:22. Bowen ties a spouse's symptom to the reciprocity itself.

#### Kerr

- <a id="RF11"></a>**RF11** “Examples included overadequate and inadequate (one did everything right and could cope and the other did everything wrong and could not cope), decisive and indecisive (one made all the decisions and the other felt incapable of making any decisions), dominant and submissive (one led and the other followed)” Kerr, FE1 L33. Several named pairs. Which of them the code covers is not ruled.
- <a id="RF12"></a>**RF12** “Saying that people function in reciprocal relationship to one another is a description of a phenomenon, not an explanation.” Kerr, FE1 L51. The description is what gets coded; the explanation is theory (status item 8).
- <a id="RF7"></a>**RF7** “An "overfunctioning" person shapes the attitudes, feelings, and behavior of an "underfunctioning" person as much as the underfunctioning one shapes the attitudes, feelings, and behavior of the overfunctioning one.” Kerr, FE2 L216. Neither side is the cause.
- <a id="RF8"></a>**RF8** “The overfunctioning person is typically one who feels responsible for the emotional well-being of others and who works to compensate for perceived (real or imagined) deficits in their functioning.” Kerr, FE2 L218. "Perceived (real or imagined)": the deficit need not be real.
- <a id="RF9"></a>**RF9** “The underfunctioning person, on the other hand, feels dependent on the overfunctioning one to do things that he feels reluctant or unable to do himself.” Kerr, FE2 L218.
- <a id="RF97"></a>**RF97** “The dominant one gains strength and confidence in what he thinks and feels. He is sure his compass (what he believes, values and thinks) is pointing the "right" direction. Meanwhile, his partner loses confidence in her compass. One becomes the "strong" self (really pseudo-self) and the other the "weak" self.” Kerr, FE4 L82. What is traded is pseudo-self, not solid self.

#### Havstad

- <a id="RF13"></a>**RF13** “patterns that shift it include reciprocal relationships, triangles, cutoff, conflict, emotional distance, and transfers of anxiety to weaker members.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L46. Reciprocal relationships as one of the patterns whose shift she tracked.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="RF16"></a>**RF16** “Conflict, reciprocity, and triangle/projection allow one to maintain toward moves despite the tension.” Patrick, EEM L21 (2021). His model files reciprocity with the toward moves.
- <a id="RF14"></a>**RF14** “Value space: conflict, distance, reciprocal functioning, child-focus, cutoff, inside (triangle), outside (triangle), defined-self.” Patrick, WP L51 (2025). One kind of move, not two.
- <a id="RF15"></a>**RF15** “Relationship shifts are automatic moves that a person makes in relation to one or more other people. "Automatic" means that it was driven by emotion, without choice, often with short-term motivation versus long-term thoughtfulness.” Patrick, WP L53 (2025). The source of the coders' "choice" test (status item 9).

### 2b. How it shows, and what hides it

#### Bowen

- <a id="RF19"></a>**RF19** “An example is a father who has no problem in important decisions at work but who can end up in an emotional, paralyzing deadlock when he and the mother try to decide which movie to see.” Bowen, FTiCP ch3 L17. The position shows in one relationship and not another.
- <a id="RF18"></a>**RF18** “The overadequate one sees self as being forced to take responsibility and the other as a shirker. The inadequate one sees self as being "forced to submit" and the other as "dominating."” Bowen, FTiCP ch4 L56. Each side's complaint mirrors the other's.
- <a id="RF100"></a>**RF100** “The one who makes a decision for the two of them immediately becomes the overadequate one” Bowen, FTiCP ch5 L29. Who decides for the pair is one observable marker of the positions.
- <a id="RF17"></a>**RF17** “The degree of adaptiveness in one spouse is determined from the long-term functioning posture of each to the other, rather than from verbal reports. Each does some adapting to the other, and it is usual for each to believe that he or she gives in more than the other.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L171. A report of who gives in more is not the evidence.
- <a id="RF20"></a>**RF20** “this husband can be emotionally appended to his wife, and yet in his work he might be an executive, an effective executive in a big business.” Bowen, BS5 L142.

#### Kerr

- <a id="RF21"></a>**RF21** “At the extreme, the underfunctioning one may rely on the overfunctioning one to tell him how to think, feel, and act. The overfunctioning one may not experience this degree of reliance as a burden.” Kerr, FE2 L218. Exhaustion is not a required sign of overfunctioning.
- <a id="RF22"></a>**RF22** “An underfunctioning person, for example, may experience an erosion of confidence, have difficulty concentrating except on simple tasks, and view himself as a burden to others. An overfunctioning person, on the other hand, may derive strength and confidence from the dependency of others on him.” Kerr, FE2 L220.
- <a id="RF94"></a>**RF94** “An underfunctioning person may get sick by withdrawing into a self-absorbed and helpless state. This self-absorption is not the product of selfishness, but of having given up too much self-control.” Kerr, FE2 L220.
- <a id="RF25"></a>**RF25** “Functional level may be higher at work than it is at home. A parent's functional level may either increase or decrease after the birth of a child.” Kerr, FE4 L64.
- <a id="RF26"></a>**RF26** “The fixer's Achilles heel is underestimating the resources of the people he intends to "help." In the process he can create a dependence in others that undermines their functioning.” Kerr, FE4 L110.
- <a id="RF23"></a>**RF23** “Even though the bossy overfunctioning person is often perceived by himself and others as "strong" and the helpless underfunctioning person as "weak," both people seek emotional support and acceptance with about the same intensity.” Kerr, FE5 L69.
- <a id="RF24"></a>**RF24** “He monitors, even hovers over, his spouse for signs of distress and inadequacy in her functioning. He "diagnoses" his mate's emotional state more on the basis of his subjective interpretations than on what his spouse actually says.” Kerr, FE7 L81. The overfunctioner's account of the other is itself part of the pattern.


### 2c. What else it might be

#### Bowen

- <a id="RF30"></a>**RF30** “we would postulate that a fairly normal family is one in which parents can function in either the strong or the weak position, according to the demands of the situation, without threatening either one.” Bowen, FTiCP ch3 L55.
- <a id="RF29"></a>**RF29** “On one level this is a smooth working, flexible, reciprocating mechanism in which one member automatically overfunctions to compensate for the dysfunction of the other who is temporarily ill. Then there are the more chronic and fixed states of overfunctioning and dysfunction in which flexibility is lost.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35. Bowen uses the word for the flexible version too.
- <a id="RF28"></a>**RF28** “For instance, the underfunctioning of a family member who is temporarily ill will be automatically compensated by other family members who overfunction until the sick one recovers.” Bowen, FTiCP ch12 L7.
- <a id="RF101"></a>**RF101** “I discovered I was being overresponsible for the staff in some areas, and that I was in fact being irresponsible in my own functioning in other areas.” Bowen, FTiCP ch20 L7. Both positions can sit in one person, in different areas of life.

#### Kerr

- <a id="RF32"></a>**RF32** “If the level of stress on the family is not too great, this overfunctioning-underfunctioning reciprocity may aid more than detract from the functioning of the individuals involved.” Kerr, FE2 L224.
- <a id="RF31"></a>**RF31** “The emotional complementarity and reciprocal functioning that exist in a relationship can contribute to the harmony of the relationship and enhance the functioning of both people.” Kerr, FE7 L49.
- <a id="RF34"></a>**RF34** “A harmonious "togetherness" can exist between an overfunctioning spouse and dysfunctional spouse, but there actually tends to be more emotional distance in a marriage with chronic symptoms than in one without chronic symptoms.” Kerr, FE7 L65. The same pair can carry distance too.
- <a id="RF33"></a>**RF33** “Anger and blame indicate that, although an overfunctioning person is acting a little differently, he is still fused with the underfunctioning person, still "stuck" to that person emotionally.” Kerr, FE9 L127 (footnote 1). Doing less is not by itself a move out of the pattern.

### 2d. Functioning up or functioning down? (undecided; status item 5)

#### Bowen

- <a id="RF37"></a>**RF37** “the overadequate husband who might function well in his work at perhaps 55 on strength from a wife housebound with phobias, excessive drinking, or arthritis and a functioning level of 15” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L65.
- <a id="RF35"></a>**RF35** “And then I would say that this one is over-functioning by this much. And this one is under-functioning by this much. But these have a basic level of self which would be about here.” Bowen, BS1 L465. Both are measured from a shared basic level.
- <a id="RF36"></a>**RF36** “before marriage they were on equal levels of functioning, and then after the marriage he's functioning very well. ... And that he is over-functioning and she is under-functioning.” Bowen, BS3 L84. A shift dated to an event, the marriage.

#### Kerr

- <a id="RF38"></a>**RF38** “In fact, there are times when the overfunctioner loses more self to the process than the underfunctioner.” Kerr, FE3 L168. A rise in functional level can come with a loss of self.
- <a id="RF95"></a>**RF95** “two people with basic levels of 35 who marry might, during the course of the marriage, do enough "borrowing" and "trading" of "selves" that one spouse's functional level rises to an average of 55 and the other's drops to an average of 15 .” Kerr, FE4 L58. One goes up exactly as the other goes down.
- <a id="RF96"></a>**RF96** “The lower the basic level, the more the functional level will fluctuate widely and the more there will be significant discrepancies between the functional levels of closely involved people (seesaw effect).” Kerr, FE4 L60.
- <a id="RF10"></a>**RF10** “One outcome of this process is overfunctioning by certain people (functional level above basic level) and underfunctioning by others (functional level below basic level).” Kerr, FE4 L62. The one definition by level in the texts. It makes overfunctioning a rise against the person's own basic level (option (a) of status item 5).

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="RF39"></a>**RF39** “which increases anxiety the person in the overfunctioning position. This anxious, overfunctioning person is now more vulnerable to physiological and mental symptoms” Patrick, CI17 L42 (2017). "anxiety the person" [sic]. The basis cited for option (b).
- <a id="RF40"></a>**RF40** “Function is how well balanced emotion and thinking is towards the person's goals, defined/stated when they are at their calmest.” Patrick, WP L59 (2025). Functioning is measured against the person's own goals, not against the partner's.

### 2e. Who is "the adaptive one"? (undecided; status item 6)

#### Bowen

- <a id="RF43"></a>**RF43** “It also provides a notion about the more adaptive spouse who is usually the more passive. The adaptive one is much more than one who "gives in" on a controlled surface level.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L89.
- <a id="RF42"></a>**RF42** “the spouse becomes the dominant one, and the other the adaptive one, who is "programmed" to support the more dominant decision-making spouse. The adaptive spouse becomes a functional "no-self."” Bowen, FTiCP ch12 L19.
- <a id="RF41"></a>**RF41** “The one who functions for long periods in the adaptive position gradually loses the ability to function and make decisions for self.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L171. Here the adaptive one is the one who ends up underfunctioning.

#### Kerr

- <a id="RF46"></a>**RF46** “In time, the overfunctioning one can "absorb" a disproportionate amount of the family problem. As the process progresses, she feels increasingly overloaded, overwhelmed, and unsupported, and may then begin to experience the onset of phobic symptoms.” Kerr, FE2 L224. Chapter 2: the overfunctioner absorbs.
- <a id="RF44"></a>**RF44** “This overfunctioning of the less adaptive one that operates in reciprocal relationship to the underfunctioning of the more adaptive one is an example of how the process can place constraints on both people.” Kerr, FE3 L168. Chapter 3: the more adaptive one underfunctions.
- <a id="RF45"></a>**RF45** “The one making the most adjustments may be an overfunctioning person who, feeling an exaggerated sense of responsibility about making things "right" for others, is trying to do too much, or it may be an underfunctioning person who, feeling little confidence in his ability to make decisions, is depending on others too much.” Kerr, FE7 L51. Chapter 7: either side.
- <a id="RF47"></a>**RF47** “In other marriages one spouse acts so "weak" and "helpless' that the other spouse is forced into a more dominant role than he or she really wants. The overfunctioning or "dominant" one frequently becomes symptomatic in such marriages.” Kerr, FE7 L85.

### 2f. Stress, symptoms and reversal

#### Bowen

- <a id="RF48"></a>**RF48** “When the mechanism advances beyond a certain point, anxiety drives the mechanism toward panic and rapid increase in both overfunction and dysfunction.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35.
- <a id="RF51"></a>**RF51** “The sick or invalided one is too impaired to begin to regain function with an overfunctioning spouse on whom he or she is dependent. This mechanism is amazingly effective in absorbing the undifferentiation.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L171.
- <a id="RF49"></a>**RF49** “So as anxiety episodes go along, one spouse dysfunctions more and the other functions better. Until this one is so dysfunctional that they develop illness.” Bowen, BS5 L187.
- <a id="RF50"></a>**RF50** “There are a few times in which you can find dysfunction in both spouses. For instance, the over-functional one does get sick and when the over-functioning one gets sick, it can stir up some interesting changes in the pattern.” Bowen, BS5 L190. For Bowen the overfunctioner falling ill is the exception.

#### Kerr

- <a id="RF53"></a>**RF53** “An underfunctioning person who has been chronically ill many improve his functioning and experience a dramatic remission of symptoms if his overfunctioning partner becomes ill or dies.” Kerr, FE2 L220. "many" [sic], for "may". A possibility, not a rule; RF54 states the opposite outcome.
- <a id="RF54"></a>**RF54** “This is the kind of situation, however, where if one partner dies, the other's functioning may then rapidly decline.” Kerr, FE3 L143.
- <a id="RF57"></a>**RF57** “It appears that the process of reciprocal functioning may be just as important in internal bodily processes as it is in the family relationship system. There is inadequate statistical support for this assumption” Kerr, FE5 L132 (footnote 5). Kerr's own caveat.
- <a id="RF56"></a>**RF56** “The nature of this functional reciprocity becomes evident in situations where one or both parents become dysfunctional and the child's functioning improves dramatically.” Kerr, FE6 L55. Parent and child, not only spouses.
- <a id="RF52"></a>**RF52** “While a dominant-subordinate or overfunctioning-underfunctioning reciprocity in a relationship is an important mechanism for binding anxiety and stabilizing the functioning of both people, an increase in the levels of chronic anxiety can exaggerate this pattern to the point that one person's functioning is so impaired that symptoms develop.” Kerr, FE7 L51.
- <a id="RF55"></a>**RF55** “When a divorce occurs, the "sick" spouse may recover dramatically from whatever physical or emotional illness was present and the "well" spouse may suddenly or gradually collapse” Kerr, FE7 L87.

- <a id="RF64"></a>**RF64** “If this parent, sibling, or other person can stay in contact with the poorly functioning one and maintain his own functioning, for example, by not assuming excessive responsibility for the dysfunctional one, the dysfunctional person will often improve.” Kerr, FE4 L74. #### Coders' group (IRR)

- <a id="RF84"></a>**RF84** “Michael has always been a bit more laid back, I guess, but I feel like since mom's diagnosis, he's really taken a step back.” Sarah's statement as read out, IRR M1 L321.

#### Questions

The coders gave four codes (status item 11). R-0433 made this two functioning events; R-0510 rescinded that (status items 1, 3). "Doesn't really help" is Sarah's side of a mirror complaint (RF18); "exhausting" is not a required sign (RF21). "Always ... laid back" is a baseline; "since mom's diagnosis ... a step back" is dated. Is the step back distance, underfunctioning, or an away move? Is Sarah's doing more automatic, or a response to a real need (RF70)?

### 4c. Kerr's retired couple: symptoms that alternate

- <a id="RF87"></a>**RF87** “When he was sick, the wife's blood pressure was normal, her colon was quiet, and the phobias were reduced to the point that she could take care of things her husband used to take care of "because" she was phobic. - <a id="RF88"></a>**RF88** “It is as if this "dominant-symptomatic" spouse has "chosen" to support the functioning of the mate.” Kerr, FE7 L112. Kerr's reading: the symptomatic wife was the dominant one.

#### Questions

Each cycle is a pair of opposite shifts: his symptom up with her symptom down and functioning up, then the reverse. None is dated. Kerr's dominance reading has no dominance data given; is it recorded as a fact, or as a hypothesis (status item 8)?

### 4f. Havstad's case: the husband's sponsor dies
