# Anxiety (A)

For coders: what the original authors wrote about anxiety, where to find it, and what is decided. Read it for food for thought while coding. It is not a rule book; section 1 says which rules exist.

- Built 2026-09-26 from the sources listed in [`README.md`](README.md). Every quote below was checked word for word against its source file by the quote checker; the check is repeatable.
- This is the public edition: excerpts from confidential sources are left out, and so are the paths to the source files ([`README.md`](README.md)).
- Ids (A1, A2, ...) are stable. Location keys (FE5 L9, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:02:23, ...) are explained in [`README.md`](README.md).
- Transcripts of recordings are machine transcriptions. A word marked [sic] is as transcribed.
- Bowen's, Kerr's and Havstad's statements are ideas. None was tested with counted data unless the note says so.

## Coder summary

The core rule is settled: an anxiety shift needs its own evidence and is never read off a relationship code, an event, or a symptom (status items 2, 6, 10). A reading drawn from theory (e.g. "he withdrew, so his anxiety must be up") stays labelled a hypothesis, never a fact (status item 9). Which definition of anxiety applies is still undecided among three candidates; what counts as raw evidence, and how to tell acute from chronic, are open for the IRR group to settle (status items 7, 10, 11). Order between an anxiety shift and a relationship shift is never assumed, only the dates are recorded (status item 8).

The passages that matter most:
- A1: the standard definition, anxiety as the response to a threat, real or imagined.
- A51: only relative shifts are recorded, never an absolute level.
- A102/A104: the ruling that ended reading anxiety off a relationship move: the variables must be able to vary independently, or the anxiety variable says nothing.
- A24, A28, A32: a calm appearance is not evidence of anxiety down; a person's own awareness of anxiety and the anxiety itself can part company.
- R-0424 (status item 2): the interference test that keeps a symptom and an anxiety shift from being coded off the same fact twice.

The main off-theory trap: treating a relationship move (distancing, conflict, a symptom appearing) as proof by itself that anxiety went up. Every source that address this directly says otherwise, and it was rescinded as a coding rule 2026-09-26 (status item 6). An anxiety shift always needs a separate piece of evidence, even if that evidence is thin.

## 1. Status of the code

### Patrick (writing and rulings)

1. **Record a shift, never a level.** RULED. Ruling R-0054 (records shifts, not levels) — see the private edition. Patrick's draft says the same, A51.
2. **Anxiety or symptom?** RULED. Ruling R-0424 (the interference test for a symptom) — see the private edition. The coders applied it in A115.
3. **The coach infers.** RULED, then qualified. Ruling R-0427 (the coach may infer anxiety; the user can correct it) — see the private edition. After item 9, such an inference is a hypothesis until the person confirms a fact (Claude's reading, not ruled).
4. **Anxiety inside the projection code.** Ruling R-0435 (when the projection code applies) — see the private edition. Whether the parent's anxiety or the child's problem comes first is OPEN between two options (OPEN_QUESTIONS 3).
5. **Every coding rule here is provisional.** Ruling R-0511 (coding rules are provisional while ground truth is rebuilt) — see the private edition. Ground truth comes from the IRR group (R-0509).
6. **An anxiety shift needs its own evidence. Never read it off a relationship code.** RULED 2026-09-26. See the private corpus for the app's decision on this point (2026-09-26). (OPEN_QUESTIONS 1). The coders' argument that led here is A102 and A104.
7. **What counts as raw evidence of an anxiety shift?** OPEN, for the IRR group to settle (OPEN_QUESTIONS 1; IRR results, unresolved item 1). See the private corpus for the app's decision on this point (2026-09-26).. Candidates in the sources, none ruled:
   - the intensity and fixedness of the relationship patterns (A25). This reads anxiety from the moves, the circularity item 6 forbids;
   - objective signs such as restlessness or not sleeping (A24);
   - the person's own report of uncertainty and urgency (A10, A11);
   - how the person thinks, not what they think, e.g. calm but jumping from topic to topic (A50);
8. **Which came first, the anxiety shift or the relationship shift?** RULED 2026-09-26: no default. Record the order of the dates only (OPEN_QUESTIONS 2). Section 2g lists what each author claimed; each claim is a hypothesis.
9. **A reading from theory stays apart from the facts.** RULED 2026-09-26. See the private corpus for the app's decision on this point (2026-09-26). (OPEN_QUESTIONS 5). So "he withdrew, so his anxiety must have gone up" is kept, if at all, as a labelled hypothesis.
10. **Which definition?** UNDECIDED between three options (OPEN_QUESTIONS 8): (a) a sufficient combination of uncertainty and urgency (A10, A11); (b) autonomic threat response, set by perceived threat (A12); (c) both, (a) naming the conditions and (b) the response. Bowen and Kerr: a response to a threat, real or imagined (A1, A2).
11. **Acute or chronic?** OPEN. Every source separates them (section 2b). None gives a rule for telling them apart in a record. Havstad applied her acute label years after the interviews (A22).

### Coders' group (IRR)

12. **Is anxiety coded apart from functioning?** Not ruled; the coders discussed it in A118. An anxiety-up-means-functioning-down rule in the old IRR guidelines was Claude's, not a ruling (REFERENCE section c).

## 2. What the original authors wrote

### 2a. What anxiety is

#### Bowen

- <a id="A9"></a>**A9** “The Bowen theory involves two main variables. One is the degree of anxiety, and the other is the degree of integration of self.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L107. Bowen's two variables. SARF's four are a recording layer on top of them.
- <a id="A8"></a>**A8** “anxiety is the emotional responsiveness of a person to situational stress” Bowen, BS5 L124.
- <a id="A2"></a>**A2** “For operational purposes here at Georgetown, I've defined anxiety in a way that sort of fits with the theory. You see, there's so many different definitions of anxiety. So I've defined anxiety as emotional reactiveness to a real or imagined threat.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:02:23. Bowen offers it as a working definition, one among many.
- <a id="A5"></a>**A5** “I would define the reactivity as a kind of a manifestation of the anxiety, the way we see it. So theoretically both would be essentially the same. I think the notion of reactivity gives us something we can get our hands on and see better than this vague notion of anxiety.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:08:29. What a coder can see is reactivity; anxiety is read through it.

#### Kerr

- <a id="A1"></a>**A1** “Anxiety can be defined as the response of an organism to a threat, real or imagined.” Kerr, FE5 L9. The definition every later source quotes.
- <a id="A3"></a>**A3** “Reactivity is manifested along a continuum that ranges from hyperactivity (the extreme is behavioral frenzy) to hypoactivity (the extreme is behavioral paralysis).” Kerr, FE5 L9. Anxiety up can look like stillness, not only agitation.
- <a id="A4"></a>**A4** “Emotional reactivity and anxiety are processes that are not easily distinguished from one another and so the terms are usually used interchangeably in family systems theory.” Kerr, FE5 L11.
- <a id="A6"></a>**A6** “anxiety and reactivity describe different processes” Kerr, FE10 L65 (footnote 23). Kerr also says the opposite of A4; the next quote is his reason.
- <a id="A7"></a>**A7** “Emotional reactivity is not always linked to anxiety, however. Hunger and sexual attraction, for example” Kerr, FE10 L65 (footnote 23). A reaction is not by itself evidence of anxiety.

#### Havstad

- <a id="A13"></a>**A13** “tracks the relationship in time between shifts in the family system, shifts in the research subjects' anxiety and functioning levels” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L21. Anxiety as one of the tracked variables.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="A11"></a>**A11** “Anxiety is an increase in energy aimed at finding a solution to a problem. Anxiety does not occur when a person is certain about the problem. Anxiety is the behavior that inevitably occurs with an increase in both unsureness and urgency.” Patrick, TDTA L56 (2021). A person certain about the problem is not anxious under this definition.
- <a id="A10"></a>**A10** “the particular automatic, i.e., "biological" response to a sufficient combination of uncertainty and urgency. This response can be called anxiety.” Patrick, ANX L59. Look for both uncertainty and urgency. How much of each is "sufficient" is not stated.
- <a id="A12"></a>**A12** “Anxiety is synonymous with autonomic threat response. Threat response is determined by perceived threat. ... Value space: up, down, same” Patrick, SARF white paper WP L45 (2025). It does not mention uncertainty or urgency.

### 2b. Acute and chronic

#### Bowen

- <a id="A20"></a>**A20** “Regression occurs in response to chronic sustained anxiety, and not in response to acute anxiety. If there is regression with acute anxiety, it disappears when the anxiety subsides.” Bowen, FTiCP ch13 L31. Duration is the only difference a record could show.
- <a id="A19"></a>**A19** “All organisms are reasonably adaptable to acute anxiety” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L107.
- <a id="A18"></a>**A18** “Now, acute anxiety is something that comes suddenly, and it is more in the realm of the real threats than the imagined threats. ... Chronic anxiety comes more out of the imagined threats. It's the poorly differentiated people that has all these fears and concerns of what might be.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:10:12. "More", a leaning, not a sorting rule.

#### Kerr

- <a id="A14"></a>**A14** “Acute anxiety generally occurs in response to real threats and is experienced as time-limited.” Kerr, FE5 L13.
- <a id="A15"></a>**A15** “Chronic anxiety generally occurs in response to imagined threats and is not experienced as timelimited.” Kerr, FE5 L13.
- <a id="A16"></a>**A16** “Acute anxiety is fed by fear of what is; chronic anxiety is fed by fear of what might be.” Kerr, FE5 L13.
- <a id="A17"></a>**A17** “People usually adapt to acute anxiety fairly successfully.” Kerr, FE5 L13.
- <a id="A21"></a>**A21** “While specific events or issues are usually the principal generators of acute anxiety, the principal generators of chronic anxiety are people's reactions to a disturbance in the balance of a relationship system.” Kerr, FE5 L15.

#### Havstad

- <a id="A22"></a>**A22** “And for 22 of the 28 women, this involved the shift in the family system produced a decrease in chronic anxiety for the subjects. And in six of these situations, the shift actually increased what I believe now was acute anxiety.” Havstad, Havstad-50thSymposium2014 @00:18:40. "What I believe now": the acute label was her later reading, not the subjects' report.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="A23"></a>**A23** “one pays attention to events where important problems must be solved quickly. ... Other events generate anxiety, like a sudden increase in persistent tension in a relationship, meeting the family of a new love interest, losing a job, losing a house, a child diagnosis, and so on. An intense argument with a child, sibling, parent, aunt/uncle, or grandparent might result in a shift up in chronic anxiety.” Patrick, ANX L191. His draft uses "chronic" for a shift after one intense argument. Compare A15.

### 2c. How anxiety shows, and what hides it

#### Bowen

- <a id="A38"></a>**A38** “The reflexes are most observable in moderate levels of tension. In low tension they are not observable, and in high tension, the reflexes are too chaotic to see much order.” Bowen, FTiCP ch18 L35.
- <a id="A145"></a>**A145** “As tension mounts there is an increasing incidence of emotional issues between people; people tend to withdraw from the group or become silent, or form cliques or alliances between faculty members, or talk or gossip about an absent faculty member. The goal is to listen to the incidence of these phenomena, rather than focusing on the content” Bowen, FTiCP ch20 L9. Bowen reads rising tension off how often these moves occur. Status item 6 forbids coding anxiety from a move; whether the incidence may count as evidence is open (OPEN_QUESTIONS 1).
- <a id="A37"></a>**A37** “easiest to see when the tension is not too high” Bowen, BS1 L289. Patterns show best at moderate tension.
- <a id="A25"></a>**A25** “The intensity of the anxiety can be judged by the intensity and fixedness of these symptoms, the relationship patterns.” Bowen, BS5 L127. Bowen reads anxiety from the patterns. Status item 6 forbids doing that as a coding rule.
- <a id="A35"></a>**A35** “because in the binding of anxiety it is non-operational at that time obesity great all of these things these bind a lot of anxiety an ulcer binds a lot of anxiety ... It is still there, and it might come bubbling out” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:13:49. Bound anxiety is "still there". Little visible anxiety is not evidence of anxiety down.
- <a id="A26"></a>**A26** “anxiety is sort of agglutinated in various spots in the family. And you can pick this up with symptoms, life postures, characteristics, you know. So you know where it is, where it's potentially present.” Bowen or Kerr (speaker unclear), BKIS-04-pt1 @00:17:52. The same method: anxiety located by where it has settled.
- <a id="A34"></a>**A34** “the withdrawn obsessive person has the same degree of problem as the effervescent hysterical person you know yeah manifest in different ways both equally but both equally involved and to assume that this person over here who looks so distant and calm in a way superficially is he just is caught up in it” Bowen (probable), BKIS-05-pt1b @00:18:20.

#### Kerr

- <a id="A27"></a>**A27** “People can be aware of feelings by virtue of feeling them. This contrasts with emotions, which are not felt. The influence of emotions must be inferred by observing what people and other organisms do and do not do in a given situation.” Kerr, FE2 L36. Emotion is inferred from what people do. The feeling a person reports is a different thing.
- <a id="A36"></a>**A36** “When a relationship is calm and in a fairly comfortable balance, the interplay of individuality and togetherness may be barely visible. The adjustments people are making to one another are so subtle and automatic that they are not obvious.” Kerr, FE3 L48.
- <a id="A47"></a>**A47** “At times of high anxiety and serious imbalance, statements people make about the situation range from the one extreme of, "I can't survive unless you respond the way I want you to," to the other extreme of, "I can't survive if I do what you want."” Kerr, FE3 L48. The only speech examples the books tie to high anxiety.
- <a id="A39"></a>**A39** “With distancing, anxiety 'disappears' and is manifested in people's avoidance of one another and of potentially upsetting subjects. If people are pushed together or try to discuss emotionally charged issues, the anxiety reappears.” Kerr, FE3 L136. A checkable sequence: distance, then forced contact, then anxiety back.
- <a id="A29"></a>**A29** “Anxiety is a far deeper process than that coveyed by the statement, "I feel anxious." Most anxiety is not conscious.” Kerr, FE3 L192 (footnote 13; "coveyed" [sic]).
- <a id="A24"></a>**A24** “Subjective manifestations include a heightened sense of awareness and fear of impending disaster. Objective manifestations include increased responsiveness, restlessness, and autonomic nervous system changes, such as increased heart rate and blood pressure” Kerr, FE5 L11. The one list of signs in the books. The objective ones are what a person or a relative might report.
- <a id="A44"></a>**A44** “The anxiety could also be played out in chronic anger about not being "given" what one feels he is "owed."” Kerr, FE5 L43. Anger can be anxiety.
- <a id="A45"></a>**A45** “Dealing with uncertainty, anticipating the worst, mulling over whether one is approved of, accepted, or rejected, being preoccupied with what "should" or "ought" to be done, or with one's inadequacy, and feeling overloaded by responsibility are common elements that feed people's anxiety.” Kerr, FE5 L43. Kerr's list overlaps Patrick's "uncertainty" (A10).
- <a id="A46"></a>**A46** “When people become more anxious, the togetherness pressure increases.” Kerr, FE5 L65.
- <a id="A40"></a>**A40** “As anxiety increases, people experience a greater need for emotional contact and closeness and, in reaction to similar pressure from others, a greater need for distance and emotional insulation.” Kerr, FE5 L67. Both needs rise together, not one or the other.
- <a id="A41"></a>**A41** “Anxiety often increases feelings of being overloaded, overwhelmed, and isolated, feelings that are accompanied by the wish for someone to lean on, to be taken care of, to have responsibility lifted.” Kerr, FE5 L67.
- <a id="A42"></a>**A42** “When some people get anxious, they are more intent on getting others to do things their way.” Kerr, FE5 L69.
- <a id="A43"></a>**A43** “The mirror image of the bossy person is a person who becomes more helpless when he gets anxious.” Kerr, FE5 L69. Bossiness and helplessness can be two faces of the same rise.
- <a id="A30"></a>**A30** “The influence of these processes tends to be grossly underestimated, even by people who think of themselves as "anxious."” Kerr, FE5 L117.
- <a id="A33"></a>**A33** “Many seemingly inactive, low response people are called 'nonreactive' when, in fact, their inactivity is a way of managing a high level of emotional reactivity.” Kerr, FE5 L128 (footnote 1). Quiet can be managed high reactivity.
- <a id="A28"></a>**A28** “Subjective awareness of anxiety often does not coincide with its objective manifestations.” Kerr, FE8 L161 (footnote 16). A report of "I feel anxious" and the anxiety itself can part company.
- <a id="A31"></a>**A31** “Since there is no "meter" to measure anxiety or reactivity” Kerr, FE10 L41.
- <a id="A32"></a>**A32** “One spouse may appear calm because of his conviction that it is the other spouse who is at fault. His conviction is a form of reactivity. A parent may appear calm because he does not allow himself to believe that his son or daughter has problems.” Kerr, FE10 L41. Calm is not low anxiety.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="A49"></a>**A49** “Anxiety is driven by urgency and dissipates once a solution is found, or once real or perceived urgency decreases.” Patrick, ANX L113. A found solution, or less urgency, is a candidate sign of anxiety down.
- <a id="A51"></a>**A51** “The idea of absolute level of anxiety is only hypothetical and is currently not measurable. But the concept can be quantified in terms of relative shifts up and down. ... Relative shifts up or down must be inferred from factual observations on a timeline.” Patrick, ANX L223. Every shift tied to a factual observation on the timeline.
- <a id="A50"></a>**A50** “Beyond obvious signs of immediate distress, indicators include cognitive errors in describing the problem. This mostly includes being calm but jumping from topic to topic or staying on topic while providing contradictory facts.” Patrick, ANX L235.

#### Other

- <a id="A48"></a>**A48** “When anxiety gets too high, however, the very things we are doing to try to manage it, by virtue of their increased intensity, can make it worse. ... as tension mounts, the irritability factor increases proportionately” Tape narrator (not Bowen), PhysicalSymptomsAging-pt1 @00:10:41.

### 2d. What else it might be

#### Bowen

- <a id="A55"></a>**A55** “And symptom formation, you might call it crystallized or agglutinated anxiety. That's what a symptom is. Agglutinated anxiety.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:01:43. If a symptom is bound anxiety, coding anxiety from the same fact records one observation twice. The interference test (status item 2) decides which.

#### Kerr

- <a id="A57"></a>**A57** “The average level of chronic anxiety of a person and of a nuclear family unit parallels the basic level of differentiation” Kerr, FE5 L25. About an average level. SARF records shifts, not levels (status item 1).
- <a id="A52"></a>**A52** “So while differentiation and chronic anxiety are distinct processes” Kerr, FE5 L35. Signs of differentiation (acting on principle, focus on self) are not signs of anxiety down.
- <a id="A53"></a>**A53** “This is anxiety associated with trying to become more of a self, an anxiety of progression rather than regression.” Kerr, FE5 L113. A step toward self can raise anxiety.


### 2e. An event is not the anxiety

#### Bowen

- <a id="A60"></a>**A60** “It's fairly common for people to come through catastrophic things and adapt well, you know. and get anxious after it's over. That's fairly common.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:10:34. An anxiety shift can be dated after the event, not at it.

#### Kerr

- <a id="A59"></a>**A59** “once the balance is disturbed chronic anxiety is propagated more by people's reactions to the disturbance than by reactions to the event itself.” Kerr, FE5 L15.
- <a id="A62"></a>**A62** “His distancing isolates the wife, which further increases her anxiety” Kerr, FE5 L17. Kerr's pregnancy example: event, then his distance, then her anxiety up.
- <a id="A58"></a>**A58** “Stress refers to an event, not to a family's reactions to the event.” Kerr, FE10 L37. Record the event as an event; an anxiety shift needs its own evidence.


### 2f. Where anxiety goes

#### Bowen

- <a id="A144"></a>**A144** “Those family members who are most dependent on the drinking person are more overtly anxious than is the one who drinks.” Bowen, FTiCP ch12 L39. Anxiety is coded on whoever shows it. The symptom bearer is not assumed to be the anxious one.
- <a id="A65"></a>**A65** “Now, the family has a whole spectrum of ways of dealing with, absorbing, or sort of deactivating the anxiety. There are four principal ones. It's distance between the spouses, marital conflict, and each one of these things absorbs amounts of this anxiety. ... is sickness in the spouse, and the fourth one is projection to children.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:03:39. Bowen's four ways a nuclear family absorbs anxiety.

#### Kerr

- <a id="A64"></a>**A64** “Some of the ways anxiety can be bound within a family system were discussed in Chapter 3 - distance, conflict, and adapting to preserve relationship harmony.” Kerr, FE5 L47.
- <a id="A63"></a>**A63** “Relationships are by far the most effective anxiety binders.” Kerr, FE5 L51.
- <a id="A73"></a>**A73** “The first person infected by the anxiety of someone in distress will usually be the one who is most sensitized emotionally to that individual.” Kerr, FE5 L83.
- <a id="A68"></a>**A68** “Anxiety can be reduced in many ways that do not depend on the development of more of a self. For example, physical distance from emotionally significant others or denial of one's feelings responsiveness to others can provide emotional insulation” Kerr, FE5 L99. Anxiety down after distance or a cutoff is expected. It does not show more self.
- <a id="A69"></a>**A69** “Commonly, one person's symptom will stabilize or even disappear when his mate or another emotionally significant figure develops a symptom.” Kerr, FE5 L103.
- <a id="A70"></a>**A70** “The occurrence of symptom exchanges means that the basic level of chronic anxiety or emotional problem in a family has not changed; the anxiety or undifferentiation has simply become "agglutinated" or bound in a new place.” Kerr, FE5 L105. One person's relief is not the family's.
- <a id="A66"></a>**A66** “When a marriage is conflictual, family anxiety is being absorbed in the relationship between husband and wife. When a child is physically, emotionally, or socially dysfunctional, anxiety is being absorbed in the child's impaired functioning.” Kerr, FE7 L11.
- <a id="A74"></a>**A74** “Once symptoms emerge they become their own source of anxiety, both for the symptomatic individual and for the family.” Kerr, FE7 L73. A symptom can come before an anxiety shift.
- <a id="A75"></a>**A75** “Anxious family members may become overinvolved with the symptomatic person in a frenzied way or they may become underinvolved in an equally reactive way.” Kerr, FE7 L73.
- <a id="A67"></a>**A67** “The words undifferentiation and anxiety are used interchangeably in reference to what is” Kerr, FE7 L280 (footnote 1). "Anxiety" in these passages can mean undifferentiation.

#### Havstad

- <a id="A71"></a>**A71** “Anxiety shifted from one to another in the family as a function of the automatic ways the family managed emotional discomfort.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L25.

### 2g. Order and timing: claims, never defaults

Status item 8 applies to every line here: record the order of the dates, and keep any direction as a hypothesis.

#### Bowen

- <a id="A146"></a>**A146** “Mother would become anxious and then her thinking would focus on the sickness in the patient. The timing of this seemed related to the mother's own functioning rather than to the reality of the patient's functioning ... Very soon the mother's anxiety would be less and the patient's psychotic symptoms would be increased.” Bowen, FTiCP ch1 L23. One person's anxiety, then another's symptom, within hours. Sample: 3 to 5 mother-daughter pairs on the NIMH ward, chosen for the most intense attachments; no counts.
- <a id="A101"></a>**A101** “A change from a calm to a conflictual relationship might be explained by the wife as, 'The time I began to stand up to him,' when it would in fact be timed exactly with a disturbance in an extended family.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L87. Date every shift against outside events. The family's own explanation is a statement, not the order.
- <a id="A82"></a>**A82** “The higher the anxiety, the more intense the automatic triangling in the system.” Bowen, FTiCP ch14 L91. Anxiety first, then triangling.
- <a id="A100"></a>**A100** “A less integrated family may show little reaction at the time and respond later with symptoms of physical illness, emotional illness, or social misbehavior.” Bowen, FTiCP ch15 L13. Little reaction at the time is not no reaction.
- <a id="A93"></a>**A93** “When anxiety increases and remains chronic for a certain period, the organism develops tension, either within itself or in the relationship system, and the tension results in symptoms or dysfunction or sickness.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L107. "A certain period" is never given.
- <a id="A81"></a>**A81** “The process begins with anxiety in the mother.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L185. Bowen's start for projection. R-0435 codes the other start (status item 4).
- <a id="A94"></a>**A94** “But increase the anxiety and it moves toward symptom development in the dysfunctional spouse.” Bowen, BS5 L72.
- <a id="A83"></a>**A83** “and for all of us when the anxiety gets up we are vulnerable for making a togetherness move a togetherness decision which is alright I mean it happens to all of us all the time” Bowen, BKIS-06-pt2a @00:26:32. "Vulnerable": more likely, not certain.

#### Kerr

- <a id="A99"></a>**A99** “overfunctioning by certain people” Kerr, FE4 L62. Rising anxiety moves some people's functioning up, not only down.
- <a id="A95"></a>**A95** “Symptom development, therefore, depends on the amount of stress and on the adaptiveness of the individual or family to stress.” Kerr, FE5 L5. Symptoms depend on stress and adaptiveness, not on anxiety alone.
- <a id="A79"></a>**A79** “Anxiety rubs off on people; it is transmitted and absorbed without thinking.” Kerr, FE5 L29.
- <a id="A80"></a>**A80** “This adds to the mother's anxiety, which may then shift off that which distressed her initially and focus on the child's emerging problems.” Kerr, FE5 L85. The mother's anxiety first, then the child's, then the focus moves.
- <a id="A78"></a>**A78** “Little is known about the actual mechanics of this process” Kerr, FE5 L130 (footnote 2). The mechanism of spread is unknown.
- <a id="A76"></a>**A76** “Who gets anxious first is less important than the process of one person's anxiety infecting another and a vicious circle ensuing.” Kerr, FE7 L281 (footnote 2). Kerr declines to fix a direction.
- <a id="A77"></a>**A77** “It can be debated whether it is necessary to be less reactive emotionally in order to think more clearly about relationships or it is necessary to think more clearly about relationships in order to be less reactive emotionally.” Kerr, FE10 L65 (footnote 19). He leaves this order open too.
- <a id="A97"></a>**A97** “psychosis being one of the most interesting examples that the hallucinations the delusions everything could beautifully parallel the level of anxiety in the family and once that anxiety starts to come down those hallucinations come down with it” Kerr (probable), BKIS-04-pt2 @00:26:37. A clinical impression, no cases or dates given.

#### Havstad

- <a id="A96"></a>**A96** “the course of symptoms and their exacerbation and remission in many kinds of clinical illness occur in concert with shifts in the family emotional system of the patient.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L27. The clinical hypothesis the SARF variables were built to test.
- <a id="A86"></a>**A86** “family system shifts that led to shifts in subjects' anxiety levels preceded the unusual phenomenon of losing all excess weight” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L31. Relationship first, from a retrospective study of 28 women.
- <a id="A89"></a>**A89** “Those subjects all gave evidence of shifts in their family system that occurred between four to twelve weeks before they initiated their successful diet effort. The subjects themselves did not make a connection between these family system shifts and emotional shifts in themselves” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L43. The link between the shift and the subject's state is the researchers' reading.
- <a id="A87"></a>**A87** “shifts in the subjects' functional level of self, along with changes in their anxiety level, often followed the shift in the family system that preceded their diet to goal weight.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L54.

### 4a. Sarah: "feeling really anxious" (IRR synthetic case)

Sarah is a synthetic persona written by an LLM, not a client (case card irr-synthetic-sarah-arthur).

#### Coders' group (IRR)

- <a id="A124"></a>**A124** “Sarah, trouble sleeping, symptom up, anxiety up.” Patrick reporting the coders' codes, IRR M1 L149.

#### Questions

Not sleeping interferes with the body, so it passes the symptom test (status item 2). "Feeling really anxious" is a self-report: one of the candidate kinds of evidence (status item 7), and Kerr warns that awareness and anxiety can part company (A28). The diagnosis is an event, not an anxiety shift (A58). "Got a lot worse about six months ago" dates the change, not its start.
