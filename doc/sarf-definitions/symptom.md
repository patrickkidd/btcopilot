# Symptom (S)

For coders: what the original authors wrote about symptoms, where to find it, and what is decided. Read it for food for thought while coding. It is not a rule book; section 1 says which rules exist.

- Built 2026-09-26 from the sources listed in [`README.md`](README.md). Every quote below was checked word for word against its source file by the quote checker; the check is repeatable.
- This is the public edition: excerpts from confidential sources are left out, and so are the paths to the source files ([`README.md`](README.md)).
- Ids (S1, S2, ...) are stable. Location keys (FE9 L5, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:01:43, ...) are explained in [`README.md`](README.md). The anxiety page's entries are cited as "anxiety page, A70".
- Transcripts of recordings are machine transcriptions. A word marked [sic] is as transcribed.
- Bowen's, Kerr's and Havstad's statements are ideas. None was tested with counted data unless the note says so.

## Coder summary

The edge of the code is ruled: [ruling text — see the private edition] it is anxiety or functioning (status item 3). A stated diagnosis is a symptom-up shift on the person it happened to, dated to the diagnosis (status item 4); two symptoms in one breath are one shift (status item 5). A symptom is never evidence of an anxiety shift, and a reading from theory stays a labelled hypothesis (status items 8, 9). No order between a symptom and an event or a relationship shift is assumed; only the dates are recorded (status item 10). Over- and underfunctioning have no code (status item 6). How much interference counts, and whether the presenting problem is a symptom, are open for the IRR group (status items 15, 16).

The passages that matter most:
- S1, S10: the threshold and the severity of a symptom are read from impaired functioning, not from its name.
- S33, S37: an event is one fact, the family's reactions to it are others.
- S62: when a symptom moves to another person, the family's problem has not changed.
- S68, S75: a remembered order can be wrong within weeks; a close date is "suggestive", not proof.
- S17 and section 3a: a trait is not a symptom; what is recorded is a dated rise.

The main off-theory trap is a chain: any distress, or the complaint that brought the person in, is coded as a symptom; the symptom is read as proof that anxiety went up; and the nearest event or relationship move is read as its cause. Each step skips a rule (status items 3, 8, 10).

## 1. Status of the code

### Patrick (writing and rulings)

1. **Record a shift, never a level.** RULED. Ruling R-0054 (records shifts, not levels) — see the private edition. A symptom a person has "always" had is a standing condition; what is coded is a dated change in it.
2. **An event is not a symptom shift.** RULED. Ruling R-0375 (a structural event is not itself the shift) — see the private edition. The event and any symptom shift near it are separate entries (S98). Which events open a period is OPEN (status item 12).
3. **Symptom or not? The interference test.** RULED. Ruling R-0424 (the interference test for a symptom) — see the private edition. The coders reached it on a live statement (S84).
4. **A stated diagnosis.** RULED. Ruling R-0425 (a stated diagnosis is dated as a symptom-up shift) — see the private edition. Dated to the diagnosis, not to the day it is mentioned (worked example 4a). The date of a diagnosis and the start of the symptoms can be months apart (S74); recording both when both are given is Claude's reading, not ruled.
5. **Two symptoms in one breath.** RULED. Ruling R-0432 (two symptoms named together are one shift) — see the private edition.
6. **No over- or underfunctioning code.** RULED (rescinded 2026-09-25). Ruling R-0433 (the over/underfunctioning coding rule is rescinded) — see the private edition. So the claims in sections 2c and 2d about which position gets the symptom are theory, not codes. Whether Kerr's "adaptive one" is the overfunctioner or the one who gives in is OPEN (OPEN_QUESTIONS 10).
7. **Every coding rule here is provisional.** Ruling R-0511 (coding rules are provisional while ground truth is rebuilt) — see the private edition. Ground truth comes from the IRR group (R-0509).
8. **A symptom is not evidence of anxiety.** RULED 2026-09-26. Ruling OPEN_QUESTIONS 1 (anxiety needs its own evidence, never assumed) — see the private edition. One fact is not coded as both a symptom and an anxiety shift; status item 3 decides which it is. Anxiety after a symptom appears (S34) is coded only on its own evidence.
9. **A reading from theory stays apart from the facts.** RULED 2026-09-26. Ruling OPEN_QUESTIONS 5 (facts and theory-based readings are kept in separate layers) — see the private edition. So "the child's symptom shows the projection" or "the symptom absorbed the marriage's anxiety" is kept, if at all, as a labelled hypothesis.
10. **Which came first, the event, the relationship shift or the symptom?** RULED 2026-09-26: no default. Record the order of the dates only (OPEN_QUESTIONS 2). Section 2f lists what each author claimed; each claim is a hypothesis.
11. **"The independent clinical variable."** OPEN (OPEN_QUESTIONS 6). The white paper's "higher functioning should lessen the impact of A and R on S" (S57) is a clinical stance, not a coding rule and not a tested causal claim.
12. **What opens a period, and how near is near?** OPEN (OPEN_QUESTIONS 4, 7). Havstad counted a family shift within about three months of the symptom change (S76); Bowen's nephritis case is ten days (S68); OPEN_QUESTIONS 7 lists windows from weeks to years.
13. **Health facts.** OPEN (OPEN_QUESTIONS 19). A condition someone has, such as diabetes, "comes in as" a symptom (S94). A person field, a dated note, or an event with no shift: undecided.
14. **Relief or change?** Not ruled. SARF records shifts, not levels (status item 1), and no ruling says how a symptom relieved by a relationship (S64, S66) is told apart in the record from one that goes with a change in the person (S65, S63). The sources' tests are in section 2e.

### Coders' group (IRR)

15. **Is the presenting problem a symptom?** OPEN, for the IRR group (R-0509). Patrick was "undecided" (S91); Havstad restated his side, then gave Bowen theory's view against it (S92; anxiety page, A115). Under status item 3, the complaint that brought someone in is coded S only when it interferes (Claude's reading, not a separate ruling).
16. **How much interference counts?** Not ruled. One coder widened the code, another argued to keep it narrow (section 3a). For the IRR group (R-0509).
17. **Is "same" recorded?** Not ruled. Patrick in the coders' group: a later report that a symptom is unchanged adds a second data point (S97); a lifelong baseline with acute spikes does not get "same" (S96).

## 2. What the original authors wrote

### 2a. What a symptom is

#### Bowen

- <a id="S1"></a>**S1** “Symptoms develop when the dysfunction approaches nonfunctioning. Families often do not seek help until flexibility of the system is lost and the functioning of one member is severely impaired.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35. Bowen's threshold is a functioning threshold, the ground status item 3 stands on.
- <a id="S2"></a>**S2** “the husband doesn't notice anything about this until the wife goes into dysfunction. As long as she can keep her chronic illness and sort of pretty much do her work and stay functional, there's no problem for him. He runs into the problem when she goes into dysfunction, when she can no longer do her work.” Bowen, BS2 L1980. The chronic illness is present throughout; what changes is whether she can still do her work.
- <a id="S3"></a>**S3** “The social illnesses include drinking problems, drug problems, behavior problems, delinquency, and all kinds of failures to function in the family, in the work systems, and in social systems. And this can be anything from just inability to perform effectively, missing appointments, all kinds of things, forgetting, to the more serious things like drinking.” Bowen, BS5 L170. Whether a given instance interferes enough is the status item 3 question.

#### Kerr

- <a id="S4"></a>**S4** “Adaptiveness has been exceeded when the intensity of a person's anxious response to stress impairs his own functioning and/or the functioning of those with whom he is emotionally connected. The functional impairment can range from mild to serious physical, emotional, or social symptoms.” Kerr, FE5 L5. Compare status item 3.
- <a id="S5"></a>**S5** “The patterns that contribute to the development of physical illness are the same as those that contribute to the development of emotional or social illness. This does not mean that patterns of emotional functioning in a family cause physical, emotional, or social illness; the creation of a specific illness depends on the combination of many factors.” Kerr, FE7 L9. Which person, and which category, is section 2d.
- <a id="S6"></a>**S6** “Emotional dysfunction exists on a continuum ranging from mild neurotic symptoms that come and go with fluctuations in the level of chronic anxiety to severe psychotic symptoms that are chronic.” Kerr, FE7 L75. Physical dysfunction gets the same continuum at FE7 L89.
- <a id="S7"></a>**S7** “When evaluating the level of differentiation that a particular clinical symptom reflects, the most important parameter to consider is the degree of functional impairment associated with the symptom.” Kerr, FE7 L291 (footnote 12). The footnote's example: two identical psychotic reactions, told apart by recovery and later functioning.
- <a id="S8"></a>**S8** “A disturbance in the balance of the emotional system, both within an individual and within his relationship system, can trigger the development of symptoms. Acute symptoms are associated with short-term disturbances in the balance of a system. Chronic symptoms are associated with long-term disturbances.” Kerr, FE9 L5. The chapter's opening definition.
- <a id="S9"></a>**S9** “the primary question posed by a systems model is not, "What has caused this disease?" but, "How has the harmonious balance of relationships within the 'sick' person and within his central relationship network been disturbed?"” Kerr, FE9 L15. Kerr asks the same question again at FE9 L124.
- <a id="S10"></a>**S10** “The severity of the symptoms is described last. This can be done on a scale such as "0 to 4" or "1 to 10" or "mild to severe." This assessment is based on the detailed history of the symptoms that was obtained during the evaluation interview. Severity refers to the degree to which a person's functioning is impaired.” Kerr, FE10 L29. Severity is read from impairment, not from the diagnosis.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="S11"></a>**S11** “Symptoms are simple enough. They are the problems that bring people in to see clinicians. They encompass the entire range of mental, behavioral, emotional, and psychiatric diagnoses. They also include many medical symptoms, especially chronic and more complex or difficult to predict issues like auto-immune, allergies, cancers, etc.” Patrick, WP L39 (2025). Status items 3 and 15 decide which of these is coded.

### 2b. What else it might be

#### Bowen

- <a id="S12"></a>**S12** “Symptoms in any part of the family are viewed as evidence of dysfunction, whether the symptoms be emotional, physical, conflictual, or social.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L37. SARF records conflict as a relationship move, so a conflict alone is not an S shift. Compare Kerr (S50).
- <a id="S13"></a>**S13** “The concept of differentiation has no direct connection with the presence or absence of symptoms. ... There are also fairly well-differentiated people who can be stressed into dysfunction, but they recover rapidly.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L111. A symptom does not by itself say where a person stands on the scale.
- <a id="S130"></a>**S130** “Much of their functioning appeared to follow from an assumption that feeling helpless was equivalent to being ill, and not feeling helpless was equivalent to being healthy.” Bowen, FTiCP ch19 L11. A reported illness can be how helplessness is presented, and a denial of helplessness can delay a real one. Record who reported whose symptom. Sample: 7 ward families.
- <a id="S14"></a>**S14** “And symptom formation, you might call it crystallized or agglutinated anxiety. That's what a symptom is. Agglutinated anxiety. So if you want to fix a symptom, you de-agglutinate the anxiety.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:01:43. Also the anxiety page, A55. Bowen's theory makes a symptom a form of anxiety. The coding keeps them apart (status item 8).
- <a id="S15"></a>**S15** “I mean obsessions and compulsions. I use them all the time. Addictions. These are all anxiety binding. And if you have a good anxiety binder, wonder what's wrong. Don't try to cure it.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:14:21. Bowen puts habits and addictions on one continuum with symptoms. Status item 3 decides whether an instance is coded S.

#### Kerr

- <a id="S16"></a>**S16** “Overeating, undereating, overachieving, underachieving, excessive alcohol or drug use, and relationships such as affairs are, in part, symptoms of anxiety and attempts to manage it.” Kerr, FE3 L176. Kerr's word "symptoms" here covers behaviour that may not interfere. Status item 3 decides whether an instance is S, anxiety or functioning.
- <a id="S17"></a>**S17** “Personality traits such as obsessiveness and hysteria, impulsiveness and indecisiveness, passivity and aggressiveness ... can also serve as anxiety binders. ... In both instances, however, when stress increases, so does the obsessiveness. This is also true of underachieving, alcohol abuse, and other symptoms.” Kerr, FE5 L55. A trait and a symptom on one continuum. What is recorded is the dated rise, not the trait.
- <a id="S18"></a>**S18** “Assessment of the level of chronic anxiety or emotional reactivity in a nuclear family is based on the number of symptoms in the family, the degree of functional impairment associated with those symptoms, the amount of distance and/or conflict in relationships, and the amount of anxiety and reactivity family members appear to have.” Kerr, FE10 L41. Kerr's clinical assessment reads anxiety partly off symptoms. SARF coding does not (status item 8).

#### Havstad

- <a id="S19"></a>**S19** “Symptom change in a family member is observed as shifts in their emotional functioning that follow from shifts in the family emotional system. The symptom change is seen and documented as part of the family unit shift.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L68. Here a symptom change is observed as a functioning shift. Status item 3 keeps symptom and functioning as separate codes.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="S20"></a>**S20** “"Sally is depressed" is a theory. So is "Sally has trouble getting out of bed and speaking up at dinner. But only when at home every weekday, less on weekends, and she is hyperactive at school." The second theory is more sophisticated because it describes the relationships between a greater number of variables.” Patrick, PiN L25 (2019). A label is a theory; the dated, placed behaviour is what gets recorded.


### 2c. An event is not the symptom: how symptoms develop

#### Bowen

- <a id="S22"></a>**S22** “In another pattern, one spouse volunteers to be the "noself" in support of the other on whom they become dependent. The spouse who "loses self" in this mechanism may come to function at such a low level that they become candidates for physical, emotional, or social illness. There are some marriages that continue for years with one functioning well and the other chronically ill.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L69. A vulnerability claim. Over- and underfunctioning have no code (status item 6).
- <a id="S23"></a>**S23** “This would see a symptom as evolving when an adaptation mechanism fails, for instance. In dysfunction in a spouse. As long as this is flexible, the anxiety is not too great, then this is an effective mechanism. When the mechanism gets overloaded, then is when symptoms erupt.” Bowen, BS5 L317.

#### Kerr

- <a id="S24"></a>**S24** “An overfunctioning person may get sick by virtue of being required by others and requiring of himself more than he can realistically accomplish. An underfunctioning person may get sick by withdrawing into a self-absorbed and helpless state.” Kerr, FE2 L220. Kerr gives both positions a route to illness; Bowen makes the overfunctioner's illness the exception (S45). No code either way (status item 6).
- <a id="S25"></a>**S25** “Regardless of the level of differentiation, however, any nuclear family can be sufficiently stressed for symptoms to develop. ... So, while a well differentiated family usually has no symptoms, it can develop them under sufficient stress. In contrast, a poorly differentiated family is rarely without symptoms.” Kerr, FE7 L15.
- <a id="S26"></a>**S26** “The person most prone to becoming symptomatic is the one who makes the most adjustments in his or her thoughts, feelings, and behavior to preserve relationship harmony. The one making the most adjustments may be an overfunctioning person ... or it may be an underfunctioning person” Kerr, FE7 L51. A vulnerability claim. Over- and underfunctioning have no code (status item 6).
- <a id="S27"></a>**S27** “The severity of the symptom, in other words, tends to parallel the intensity of the relationship process that helps create the symptom.” Kerr, FE7 L75.
- <a id="S28"></a>**S28** “It is presumed that the severity of the symptom can be out of proportion to the intensity of the relationship process if a person has major physiological defects or is exposed to large amounts of potentially pathogenic influences in the environment.” Kerr, FE7 L290 (footnote 11). The exception to the line above.
- <a id="S29"></a>**S29** “Symptoms can be generated by an anxiety-driven togetherness process characterized by people's pressuring one another to think, feel, and act in specific ways. Symptoms can also be generated by disruption of a togetherness process that has sustained someone's functioning - for example, the disruption of an important relationship through death or divorce.” Kerr, FE9 L7.
- <a id="S30"></a>**S30** “However, this does not mean that the family is the cause of the problem. ... The way people behave toward one another can set the stage for a clinical problem, but all these other components of the emotional system influence whether the problem actually flowers.” Kerr, FE9 L35. Stage-setting, not cause. Compare Bowen's causal wording (S46).
- <a id="S31"></a>**S31** “When most of the anxiety is bound in a stable arrangement of relationships and activities, the emotional system is said to be "in balance." If the balance is associated with clinical symptoms, the symptoms are fairly stable. If the balance is disturbed, new symptoms may appear and/or chronic symptoms may worsen.” Kerr, FE9 L45. Two kinds of symptom up: a new symptom, or a chronic one worsening.
- <a id="S32"></a>**S32** “An event, or more likely a series of events, can disturb the balance of a relationship system and trigger symptoms. The event may be the addition of something new that has to be dealt with or the loss of something old that was relied on.” Kerr, FE9 L48.
- <a id="S33"></a>**S33** “people usually do not get isolated or overwhelmed by events, but by a relationship process that is set in motion by the events. As family members get more anxious, they lean on or withdraw from one another more. In the leaning on, retreating from, and insisting that one another change, someone often absorbs a disproportionate share of the system's anxiety.” Kerr, FE9 L52. The event and the reactions to it are separate facts with separate dates.
- <a id="S34"></a>**S34** “The development of a symptom itself can be an event that adds to a family's anxiety. For example, if a child develops a chronic medical or emotional problem, the problem may be not only a repository for a family's anxiety, but also a source of anxiety.” Kerr, FE9 L70. Also the anxiety page, A74. Anxiety after a symptom is coded only on its own evidence (status item 8).
- <a id="S35"></a>**S35** “Real or anticipated events in either extended family can disturb a previously balanced nuclear family system. ... A death of an important member of the extended family (a person who is an important stabilizing force for the family) is probably the event occurring outside a nuclear family that has the most potential to generate anxiety in a nuclear family.” Kerr, FE9 L74. "Probably" is Kerr's word. Footnote 9 adds that a family may also be calmer after a death.
- <a id="S36"></a>**S36** “A divorce by a mother's sister may be expressed as marked exacerbation of bed-wetting in the mother's youngest child. The child is not reacting to his aunt's divorce; he is reacting to his mother's anxiety. The mother's anxiety may not be "caused" by her sister's divorce, but may be more related to her husband's distancing from her in reaction to her need for more emotional reassurance.” Kerr, FE9 L74. The event and the symptom sit in different people, with a relationship move between them. None of the links is a recorded fact.
- <a id="S37"></a>**S37** “Stress refers to an event, not to a family's reactions to the event. The emotionally driven chain reactions that can be set in motion in a family relationship system in response to an event are often a much greater source of stress to a family than the event itself. This fourth component of family diagnosis, however, is confined to describing the events themselves.” Kerr, FE10 L37. Also the anxiety page, A58. Record the event as an event (status item 2).

#### Havstad

- <a id="S38"></a>**S38** “Four of the subjects dieted as a means to restore equilibrium in relationships in which their husbands had become highly critical, or they dieted to maintain equilibrium in the marriage when it was threatened, and increased anxiety motivated the effort to diet.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L43. A symptom improving after anxiety went up, in 4 of 28 women.
- <a id="S39"></a>**S39** “Bowen used the model of a hurricane to illustrate the idea that the family system is a necessary but not sufficient variable in the development of clinical symptoms. ... the family process is one important and maybe necessary factor in symptom remission. This is hidden from view for most individuals.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L118. "Maybe necessary": other variables are always at work too.

### 2d. Where a symptom sits and how it moves

#### Bowen

- <a id="S128"></a>**S128** “On the day the shifting process is finally given a diagnostic label and is officially located in the person of the patient, a big change takes place in the family. The shifting process is then more fixed and crystallized in the patient.” Bowen, FTiCP ch3 L73. The diagnosis is a symptom-up shift on the person (status item 4); Bowen treats the labelling as an event for the whole family. Whether it is also coded that way is open (OPEN_QUESTIONS 34).
- <a id="S129"></a>**S129** “Each time one family member makes a significant change, complementing changes in the others follow almost immediately. When the psychotic one improves, it is usually the mother who becomes symptomatic.” Bowen, FTiCP ch3 L91. When one person improves, check the others. "Usually" is uncounted; one outpatient family is the example.
- <a id="S132"></a>**S132** “Generally, it is a hopeful sign when acting out in the community shifts back within the family.” Bowen, FTiCP ch8 L111. The place of a symptom (school, community, home) can shift without its intensity changing. Record where each episode happened.
- <a id="S40"></a>**S40** “One of the most important aspects of family dysfunction is an equal degree of overfunction in another part of the family system. It is factual that dysfunctioning and overfunctioning exist together.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35. Later in the paragraph Bowen claims that in chronic states the dysfunction appears after the overfunction, with no evidence shown.
- <a id="S41"></a>**S41** “Within the family ego mass, spouses use three major mechanisms to control the intensity of the ego fusion. (1) Marital conflict ... (2) Dysfunction in one spouse. ... (3) Transmission of the problem to one or more children.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L69. In the Basic Series Bowen lists distance as a fourth mechanism (BS5 L17).
- <a id="S42"></a>**S42** “The fact that there are some families with intense marital conflict and no impairment of children is evidence that marital conflict does not, within itself, cause problems in children. The fact that serious impairment of children can develop in calm, harmonious marriages is further evidence that impairment of children can occur without conflict.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L69. Bowen's own argument against reading a child's symptom off the marriage's conflict.
- <a id="S43"></a>**S43** “When one person is impinged by the system, the system works better. There can be overt conflict going throughout the system, overt disharmony. And then when one person gets sick, impinged, the conflict settles down, and the illness sort of becomes solidified.” Bowen, BS2 L1483. A symptom in one person can coincide with less conflict between others.
- <a id="S44"></a>**S44** “the one that develops physical illness focuses on body or has had a body focus in life. The one who develops emotional illness has come from an environment that has focused on emotional things, attitudes, culture values. And the one who develops social illness has had a focus on behavior. That is one way of thinking about it. It is probably too simplistic” Bowen, BS5 L165. Bowen's hypothesis about which category appears, with his own caveat. Compare Kerr (S53).
- <a id="S45"></a>**S45** “So as anxiety episodes go along, one spouse dysfunctions more and the other functions better. Until this one is so dysfunctional that they develop illness. ... There are a few times in which you can find dysfunction in both spouses. For instance, the over-functional one does get sick” Bowen, BS5 L187. Bowen: the illness is characteristically in the dysfunctioning spouse; the overfunctioner falling ill is the exception. Compare Kerr (S24).
- <a id="S46"></a>**S46** “Kids are harmed by the presence of the family projection process. Now, the family projection, see we have all these things in which we say that marital conflict causes problems in kids. Don't believe it.” Bowen, BS5 L335. Bowen speaks causally here ("harmed by"); Kerr declines causal language (S30). Both are theory, not coding rules (status item 9).

#### Kerr

- <a id="S47"></a>**S47** “Once a symptom emerges, a relationship process can develop around the symptomatic person which may foster its becoming chronic. The family may become anxiously focused on the dysfunctional person. This focus on the problems of one person may create a type of togetherness that is fairly stable.” Kerr, FE3 L178.
- <a id="S48"></a>**S48** “Commonly, one person's symptom will stabilize or even disappear when his mate or another emotionally significant figure develops a symptom. Frequently, after a symptomatic child enters psychotherapy and improves, his parents will report that a sibling, previously functioning well, has developed problems.” Kerr, FE5 L103. Also the anxiety page, A69. Each person's shift is recorded on that person with its own date.
- <a id="S49"></a>**S49** “It appears that the family owns the symptom - not just the nuclear family but the multigenerational family as well. The family is the "unit of illness." ... Saying that the family is the unit of illness does not imply that the family is "sick." It only implies that the family is a system.” Kerr, FE5 L109.
- <a id="S50"></a>**S50** “Each pattern is intensified by anxiety and, at a sufficient level of intensity, contributes to the development of a particular category of clinical dysfunction. Three categories of dysfunction occur in nuclear families: (1) illness in a spouse; (2) marital conflict; and (3) impairment of one or more children.” Kerr, FE7 L5. Kerr's categories here are places in the family, and marital conflict is one of them. Compare Bowen (S12).
- <a id="S51"></a>**S51** “Where a symptom occurs in a relationship system (in which family member or in which family relationship) is determined by the particular pattern or patterns of emotional functioning that predominate in that family system.” Kerr, FE7 L7. About which person or relationship. Which category (physical, emotional, social) is S53.
- <a id="S52"></a>**S52** “The degree to which the undifferentiation of a nuclear family system is absorbed in one relationship or in the poor functioning of one person is the degree to which other relationships and other people are protected from dysfunction. If one parent's functioning is impaired, the other parent may function better in reaction to it.” Kerr, FE7 L11.
- <a id="S53"></a>**S53** “The type of symptom that develops (physical, emotional, or social) is connected both to the particular way an individual manages anxiety and to what others in the system focus on in that individual when they get anxious.” Kerr, FE7 L51.
- <a id="S54"></a>**S54** “the presence of symptoms in a child, particularly if they are life-threatening, can increase a family's level of anxiety; however, the presence of symptoms, particularly if they are not lifethreatening, can also defuse the intensity of other problems in a family.” Kerr, FE7 L247 [sic: lifethreatening].

#### Havstad

- <a id="S55"></a>**S55** “I think this operates in all kinds of symptoms in the background. I think it should be a control variable in all research that has to do with symptom development, course of symptoms, and treatments” Havstad, Havstad-50thSymposium2014 @00:27:24. "All kinds of symptoms". Patrick's white paper declines to claim all (S57).
- <a id="S56"></a>**S56** “The automatic relationship patterns relieved some family members of anxiety while it correspondingly increased for the patients, activating and exacerbating their symptoms. When the patterns shifted so that the patient had less of the anxiety and another family member had more, the patient's symptoms moderated.” Havstad and Sheffield, retelling Bowen's NIMH work, ART L25. When one person's symptom eases, look at the others.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="S57"></a>**S57** “The model does not imply that *all* symptoms work this way, which would be bad scientific practice. F is the independent clinical variable, where higher functioning should lessen the impact of A and R on S. The goal is to "function up."” Patrick, WP L21 (2025). "Independent clinical variable" is not a causal claim; open (status item 11).

### 2e. Relief is not change

#### Bowen

- <a id="S58"></a>**S58** “When the mechanism advances beyond a certain point, anxiety drives the mechanism toward panic and rapid increase in both overfunction and dysfunction. The increased pressure can "jam the circuits" of the disabled one into paralyzed collapse. Even at this point, recovery can begin with the slightest decrease of the overfunctioning, or a slight decrease in the dysfunction.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35. Recovery here starts from either side of the reciprocity; the passage names no order.
- <a id="S59"></a>**S59** “It is common for mental health professionals to consider the disappearance of symptoms as evidence of change.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L33. Bowen sets this against "basic, documentable, structural alteration in the underlying situation that gave rise to the symptoms" in the same paragraph.

#### Kerr

- <a id="S60"></a>**S60** “In December, the husband had to move temporarily to another city because of job circumstances. Within three weeks after his departure, his symptoms dramatically improved. The husband said he was a calmer person when not in the physical presence of his wife.” Kerr, FE2 L228. Relief by distance. Compare the togetherness relief (S64, S66).
- <a id="S61"></a>**S61** “Sometimes just seeking help can reduce anxiety and, consequently, symptoms. As the anxiety subsides, each person recovers some ability to act on thinking, the emotional boundaries between family members gradually return to baseline level, and symptoms diminish or disappear.” Kerr, FE5 L91.
- <a id="S62"></a>**S62** “The occurrence of symptom exchanges means that the basic level of chronic anxiety or emotional problem in a family has not changed; the anxiety or undifferentiation has simply become "agglutinated" or bound in a new place. ... In these instances, the relationship process that contributes to symptom development is bypassed in favor of symptom relief.” Kerr, FE5 L105. Also the anxiety page, A70. One person's symptom down next to another's symptom up is not a family improvement.
- <a id="S63"></a>**S63** “There can be symptoms in another family member associated with someone's effort to change, but these symptoms will disappear when the system equilibrates on a higher level of functioning. If the symptoms do not disappear, this indicates that emotional distance has been a major component of the effort to "change."” Kerr, FE5 L138 (footnote 11). A symptom in another person can follow a genuine change too. Kerr's test is whether it passes.
- <a id="S64"></a>**S64** “Symptoms can also be relieved by a togetherness process. Feeling "at one with the other" can be extraordinarily calming. ... The reduction of symptoms based on a togetherness process is related to an increase in functional level of differentiation.” Kerr, FE9 L7.
- <a id="S65"></a>**S65** “An increase in basic level can reduce symptoms without that reduction being dependent on the disruption of a relationship that erodes one's functioning or on the development of a relationship that sustains one's functioning.” Kerr, FE9 L9.
- <a id="S66"></a>**S66** “Family anxiety is often reduced in the process, but basic patterns are not changed. Such "therapy" may continue for many years. It is a togetherness "solution" to the problem, a "solution" contingent on sustaining particular relationships.” Kerr, FE9 L92.
- <a id="S67"></a>**S67** “People who are significantly cut off from their families of origin and who develop serious symptoms may get complete or at least partial remission of those symptoms based on reestablishing contact with the family. Reestablishing contact is not a matter of a few phone calls to parents or a few trips home.” Kerr, FE9 L94.

### 2f. Order and timing: claims, never defaults

Status item 10 applies to every line here: record the order of the dates, and keep any direction as a hypothesis.

#### Bowen

- <a id="S68"></a>**S68** “Ten days after the wife returned from her mother's funeral, her daughter developed nephritis. Some weeks later the wife was insisting that the daughter's illness preceded her mother's death. The husband's memory and my notes were accurate. In theoretical thinking, I have never been willing to postulate causality or go beyond noting that such events have a striking time sequence.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L93. A remembered order can be wrong within weeks. Bowen records the sequence and declines to call it cause.
- <a id="S69"></a>**S69** “The family emotional system operates always to obscure and misremember and to treat such events as coincidental. ... My goal is to keep asking questions and let the calendar "speak" when others are able to "hear."” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L93. Dates are the record; the connection between them is left for the family to see.
- <a id="S131"></a>**S131** “The symptom of excessive drinking occurs when family anxiety is high. The appearance of the symptom stirs even higher anxiety in those dependent on the one who drinks.” Bowen, FTiCP ch12 L3. Both orders in two sentences: anxiety before the symptom, and the symptom before others' anxiety. Record the dates.
- <a id="S70"></a>**S70** “The equilibrium of the unit is disturbed by either the addition of a new member or the loss of a member. ... A well integrated family may show more overt reactiveness at the moment of change but adapt to it rather quickly. A less integrated family may show little reaction at the time and respond later with symptoms of physical illness, emotional illness, or social misbehavior.” Bowen, FTiCP ch15 L13. Also the anxiety page, A100. A symptom can lag the event, and a quiet first reaction is not evidence of no reaction.
- <a id="S71"></a>**S71** “Then I want to find out the beginning of the time the pattern breaks down and when symptoms erupt. Then I want to take the symptoms as they develop on a calendar year by year as it goes from the beginning symptom until the symptom is serious enough for them to seek family therapy or some kind of help.” Bowen, BS3 L479. Each symptom dated from its start, not only the presenting one.
- <a id="S72"></a>**S72** “I wouldn't be willing to say that this death causes this, but I would be willing to say that these kinds of human problems follow a death in time sequence in a fair percentage of families.” Bowen, BS6 L119. Sequence recorded, cause not claimed. Compare S68.

#### Kerr

- <a id="S73"></a>**S73** “In the process, that person becomes more vulnerable to a symptom. If family anxiety soon returns to "pre-event" levels, the symptom will usually be short-lived. However, if family anxiety becomes bound in a chronic focus on the symptomatic one, the dysfunction will become chronic.” Kerr, FE9 L52. Kerr's order claim: short-lived unless a focus forms. A claim, not a coding default.
- <a id="S74"></a>**S74** “This case is particularly interesting in that the initial diagnosis of cancer followed the disruption of a significant relationship and the recurrence followed the initiation of a significant relationship. ... The symptoms of her cancer may have been present at least four months before the recurrence was actually diagnosed.” Kerr, FE9 L66. The date of the diagnosis and the date the symptom began can differ by months.
- <a id="S75"></a>**S75** “Fairly exact dates of when symptoms developed or recurred are important. These dates may be found to correlate with information that is gathered later in the interview. For example, a wife's first diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis may be found to have occurred three months after her husband's mother died. Such a correlation does not establish a relationship, but is suggestive of one.” Kerr, FE10 L11. A diagnosis is dated like any symptom (status item 4); a close date is suggestive, not proof.

#### Havstad

- <a id="S76"></a>**S76** “When subjects directly or indirectly provided facts about a change in the nuclear or extended group that had made a significant difference to them—and it had occurred within approximately three months prior to dieting—the event was counted as related to onset of the diet to goal weight.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L39. #### Patrick (writing and rulings)

## 5. Readings that look right but are off-theory

| The reading | What the sources say instead | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Any distress, or the complaint that brought someone in, is a symptom. | A symptom interferes with body, conduct or life with others; otherwise it is anxiety or functioning. Whether the presenting problem is a symptom is open. | status items 3, 15; S1, S84, S91 |
| A symptom shows anxiety went up, so code both from one fact. | Anxiety needs its own evidence. One fact coded twice cannot test the link. | status item 8; S14, S18, S78 |
| The event near the symptom caused it. | "Stress refers to an event." People are overwhelmed by the relationship process an event sets off, and Kerr and Bowen decline to call a close date a cause. | status items 2, 10; S33, S37, S36, S72, S75 |
| The relationship move near the symptom caused it, or the family caused it. | The family "does not cause the problems". Bowen speaks causally in one lecture. Either way it is theory, kept as a hypothesis. | status item 9; S30, S46, S42 |
| The order the family remembers is the order it happened. | Bowen's wife placed her daughter's illness before her mother's death within weeks; the notes showed the reverse. | S68, S69 |
| A symptom starts on the day it is mentioned, or on the day it was diagnosed. | A stated diagnosis is dated to the diagnosis, not to the mention. The symptoms themselves can be months older. | status item 4; S74 |
| The name of the diagnosis gives the severity. | Severity is the degree of functional impairment. | S10, S7 |
| Marital conflict is a symptom code. | Bowen and Kerr list it among symptoms or categories of dysfunction; SARF records conflict as a relationship move. | S12, S50 |
| A sensitive, obsessive or anxious person has a symptom. | A trait is on a continuum with symptoms; what is recorded is a dated rise that interferes. | status item 1; S17, S83 |
| A symptom gone means the person or the family changed. | Relief through togetherness, distance or help-seeking leaves the basic patterns as they were. A symptom that moves to another person means the family's problem has not changed. | status item 14; S59, S66, S62 |
| The symptomatic one's partner is coded as overfunctioning, or the one who gives in as underfunctioning. | Over- and underfunctioning coding is rescinded. Bowen and Kerr disagree on which position falls ill. | status item 6; S45, S24 |
| A symptom says the person is poorly differentiated. | "The concept of differentiation has no direct connection with the presence or absence of symptoms." | S13, S25 |
| A relationship shift that "made a difference" to the symptom is the one to record. | Picking the shift by its effect on the symptom builds the order into the finding. | S76, S99 |
| "Anxiety symptoms" in a case write-up is an anxiety code. | That is the author's label. Code from what the text says the person could no longer do. | worked example 4b; S106 |
