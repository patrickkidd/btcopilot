# Functioning (F)

For coders: what the original authors wrote about functioning, where to find it, and what is decided. Read it for food for thought while coding. It is not a rule book; section 1 says which rules exist.

- Built 2026-09-26 from the sources listed in [`README.md`](README.md). Every quote below was checked word for word against its source file by the quote checker; the check is repeatable.
- This is the public edition: excerpts from confidential sources are left out, and so are the paths to the source files ([`README.md`](README.md)).
- Ids (F1, F2, ...) are stable. Location keys (FE4 L55, FTiCP ch21 L35, IRR M4 @00:40:03, ...) are explained in [`README.md`](README.md).
- Transcripts of recordings are machine transcriptions. A word marked [sic] is as transcribed.
- Bowen's, Kerr's and Havstad's statements are ideas. None was tested with counted data unless the note says so.
- Several passages describe a level on Bowen's scale, not a shift. Their notes say so, because the old review used level descriptions as signs of a functioning shift.

## Coder summary

Little is settled. Ruled: record a functioning shift, never a level (status item 1). Code functioning as itself: a drop in functioning is not recorded as anxiety, and a reading from theory such as "borrowed self" stays a labelled hypothesis (status items 9, 10). No default order between a functioning shift and the other shifts; record the dates (status item 11). The functioning wording is ruled but its example is undecided, and every over- and under-functioning rule was rescinded (status items 4 to 6). Whether overfunctioning is a shift up, a shift down or no functioning code, what a defined self is, and where functional facts go are open for the IRR group (status items 13, 15, 16).

The passages that matter most:
- F4: Kerr's definition. Functional level is the part of functioning that depends on the relationship process.
- F48, F23, F20: a shift up can be borrowed from a spouse, a group or a belief. It is a real shift, not a gain in basic level.
- F19, F52: rising anxiety moves some people's functioning up and others' down.
- F10, F8: who adapts is read from what each person does over time, not from what each says.
- F58, F57: an event does not fix the direction of a shift, and a shift can last hours or years.

The main off-theory trap: reading functioning up off success or strength (weight lost, a promotion, a "model son", the one "holding everything together"), and reading functioning down off anxiety or a symptom. Success can rest on borrowed self (F24, F16). The overfunctioner may be the more vulnerable one (F30). Heavy drinking can sit beside adequate functioning (F36). Record what the person did, dated; the reading stays a hypothesis.

## 2. What the original authors wrote

### 2a. What functioning is: functional and basic level

#### Bowen

- <a id="F1"></a>**F1** “There are many life experiences that can raise or lower the functioning levels of self, but few that can change the basic level of differentiation acquired while people are still with their parental families. Unless there is some unusual circumstance, the basic level from their parental family is consolidated in a marriage, following which the only shift is a functional shift.” Bowen, FTiCP ch21 L35. In adult life the shifts a coder sees are functional shifts. "Unusual circumstance" leaves room for a change in basic level.
- <a id="F2"></a>**F2** “A number of variables govern the developments, development of symptoms in the nuclear family. The first of these has to do with the operational or functional level of differentiation of self. The second has to do with the fixed relationship patterns in the family.” Bowen, BS5 L1. Bowen names functional level and the fixed relationship patterns as separate variables governing symptoms.

#### Kerr

- <a id="F3"></a>**F3** “Associated with the capacity to distinguish between feelings and thoughts is the ability to choose between having one's functioning guided by feelings or by thoughts.” Kerr, FE4 L53. The scale's discriminator, which describes a level. R-0428's wording (emotion and intellect toward longer-term goals) draws on the same idea (status item 4).
- <a id="F4"></a>**F4** “Basic differentiation is functioning that is not dependent on the relationship process. Functional differentiation is functioning that is dependent on the relationship process. This means that people with widely different basic levels can, under some circumstances, have similar functional levels.” Kerr, FE4 L55. The definition. A functioning shift is a change in the part that depends on the relationship process. Two people at different basic levels can look alike.
- <a id="F5"></a>**F5** “People in the 35-40 range have low levels of solid self, an important component of basic differentiation, but reasonable levels of pseudo-self an important component of functional differentiation. ... Pseudo-self is created by emotional pressure and can be modified by emotional pressure.” Kerr, FE4 L78. Pseudo-self is the part emotional pressure can change, so it is what a functioning shift records.

#### Havstad

- <a id="F6"></a>**F6** “The analysis of the data from the family evaluation interview tracks the relationship in time between shifts in the family system, shifts in the research subjects' anxiety and functioning levels—also known as functional levels of self—and shifts in the course of their symptom of overweight.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L21. Functioning is tracked as dated shifts, apart from anxiety and the symptom (anxiety page, A13).

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="F7"></a>**F7** “Function is how well balanced emotion and thinking is towards the person's goals, defined/stated when they are at their calmest. When functioning is low, a person is ruled by emotion and unable to utilize their intellect to plan, problem solve, see the forest for the trees.” Patrick, WP L59 (2025). The white paper's definition; its value space is up, down, same. The goals are the ones the person states when calm. R-0428 rules a shorter wording (status item 4).

### 2b. How a functioning shift shows, and what hides it

#### Bowen

- <a id="F120"></a>**F120** “A parent might function successfully and efficiently in outside business and social relationships and yet, within the family group, find self paralyzed by indecision” Bowen, FTiCP ch2 L7. Functioning differs by setting. Record where a shift was seen (home, work).
- <a id="F8"></a>**F8** “A common pattern is a brief period of conflict followed by one spouse who reluctantly "gives in" to relieve the conflict. Both spouses usually see self as "giving in," but there is one who does more of it.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L69. Each spouse reports giving in. The one who does more of it shows in the pattern over time.
- <a id="F9"></a>**F9** “At an important event such as this, it is desirable to do a "fix" on the entire family system, including place, date, ages of each person in the household and the functioning of each, and a check on the realities in the extended families.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L89. At each important event, the functioning of every member of the household, dated.
- <a id="F10"></a>**F10** “The degree of adaptiveness in one spouse is determined from the long-term functioning posture of each to the other, rather than from verbal reports. Each does some adapting to the other, and it is usual for each to believe that he or she gives in more than the other.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L171. Read from what each does to the other over the long term, not from verbal reports.

#### Kerr

- <a id="F11"></a>**F11** “An underfunctioning person, for example, may experience an erosion of confidence, have difficulty concentrating except on simple tasks, and view himself as a burden to others. An overfunctioning person, on the other hand, may derive strength and confidence from the dependency of others on him.” Kerr, FE2 L220. The overfunctioning position can feel like strength and confidence from the inside.
- <a id="F12"></a>**F12** “If a person is functioning on "borrowed" self, it will be evident in the functioning of those around him.” Kerr, FE4 L66. The evidence for borrowed self is in the records of the people around the person.
- <a id="F13"></a>**F13** “If the functioning of one spouse has been chronically impaired for years, if the functioning of the other spouse has been largely unimpaired ... there is evidence that the nuclear family's undifferentiation has been bound primarily in the impaired functioning of one spouse.” Kerr, FE10 L37. Kerr reads where a family's problem is bound from the functioning histories of all its members together.


### 2c. What else it might be: borrowed self, success, a level

#### Bowen

- <a id="F121"></a>**F121** “In my opinion these families are not really helpless. They are functionally helpless.” Bowen, FTiCP ch5 L65. Helplessness at home is a functioning state, not a fixed trait.
- <a id="F16"></a>**F16** “Self-esteem is dependent on others. ... Success in school is oriented more to learning the system and to pleasing the teacher than to the primary goal of learning. Success in business or in social life depends more on pleasing the boss or the social leader” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L129. Bowen on the 25-50 range, a level. Success at school or work can rest on pleasing others. A promotion or good grades is not by itself a shift up.
- <a id="F122"></a>**F122** “they mistake functional shifts for basic change” Bowen, FTiCP ch17 L76. A functioning shift up is not a change in basic level.
- <a id="F17"></a>**F17** “The differentiation of self scale is an effort to assess the basic level of self in a person. The basic self is a definite quality illustrated by such "I position" stances as: "These are my beliefs and convictions. This is what I am, and who I am, and what I will do, or not do."” Bowen, FTiCP ch21 L27. These stances illustrate basic self, a level. Bowen does not give them as signs of a functional shift.
- <a id="F18"></a>**F18** “The functional shifts can be striking. ... Many of these functional levels are sufficiently consolidated so that they can appear much like basic levels to the inexperienced.” Bowen, FTiCP ch21 L35. A long-held functional position can look like a trait. Bowen still calls it functional.

#### Kerr

- <a id="F19"></a>**F19** “When anxiety is high, people can become more reactive and less thoughtful; system functioning is prone to decline. The anxiety destabilizes individuals and increases the relationship focus. One outcome of this process is overfunctioning by certain people (functional level above basic level) and underfunctioning by others (functional level below basic level).” Kerr, FE4 L62. Overfunctioning is defined as functioning above basic level, so rising anxiety can push a person's functioning up. How to code it is open (status items 6, 13).
- <a id="F20"></a>**F20** “Functional level can be enhanced by relationships, drugs, beliefs, cultural values, religious dogma, and even superstitions. It can rise and fall quickly or be stabilized over long periods, depending largely on the status of central relationships.” Kerr, FE4 L64. What can raise functional level. None of it changes basic level.
- <a id="F21"></a>**F21** “The "self" is so poorly developed that use of the pronoun "I" is confined to narcissistic pronouncements such as, "I want, I hurt, I want my rights." They are incapable of more differentiated "I" statements such as, "I believe, I am, I will do."” Kerr, FE4 L70. This describes the 0-25 basic range, a level. It is not a sign of a shift.
- <a id="F22"></a>**F22** “Functioning can improve and symptoms can be reduced if the relationship environment is favorable, but improvement is contingent on the situation and not on becoming more differentiated.” Kerr, FE4 L74. Relief is not change. A shift up in a favourable setting depends on the setting.
- <a id="F23"></a>**F23** “A group can "pump up" an individual's level of functioning to the point that he can do things he had been unable to do on his own. This higher level of functioning, however, is totally dependent on the group's continuing support. A group can also deflate an individual's functioning by focusing on his weaknesses.” Kerr, FE4 L86. A shift up can be borrowed from a group. The shift is real; it lasts while the support does.
- <a id="F24"></a>**F24** “The presence of chronic symptoms, for example, is associated with lower levels of functioning. In the 55-15 discrepancy in functional levels described above, the one at 15 may be alcoholic and housebound with severe phobias. The one at 55 may have a very responsible job and be in excellent health” Kerr, FE4 L135 (footnote 9). A responsible job and excellent health can sit on the other side of a spouse's collapse.
- <a id="F25"></a>**F25** “The husband's functioning declined after the divorce and he became depressed to the point that his professional work suffered. When he met his present wife and got engaged, however, his functioning improved dramatically. ... This history suggests that the husband gained "self" (pseudo-self) in both marriages and the wives lost "self" (pseudo-self).” Kerr, FE7 L71. Two dated shifts, down after the divorce and up at the engagement, which Kerr reads as borrowed self. The shifts are the facts; the reading is his hypothesis (status item 10).
- <a id="F26"></a>**F26** “the functional level of differentiation of one sibling can be influenced by the functional level of another sibling. For example, one child can do "everything right" in reaction to another child's doing "everything wrong."” Kerr, FE7 L298 (footnote 19). Doing well can be a reaction to someone else doing badly.

#### Havstad

- <a id="F27"></a>**F27** “And Bowen said, tell him it was automatic. ... so the question turns out to be not how did you lose the weight but what happened that you were able to lose the weight” Havstad, Havstad-50thSymposium2014 @00:16:41. The person's account of how they did it is not the evidence she looked for.
- <a id="F29"></a>**F29** “The evidence of the pilot study strongly pointed to the hypothesis that successful dieting to goal weight reflects a shift in emotional functioning of subjects in the family system.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L92. Stated as a hypothesis the evidence "pointed to", not as a finding that dieting is a functioning shift.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="F30"></a>**F30** “non-verbal coercion of one member to overfunction for an underfunctioning member which increases anxiety the person in the overfunctioning position. This anxious, overfunctioning person is now more vulnerable to physiological and mental symptoms as their internal metabolic and inflammatory reserves decrease.” Patrick, CI17 L42 (2017). "anxiety the person" [sic]. Here the overfunctioner is the more vulnerable one, not the well one. This is one option in status item 13.

### 2d. What else it might be: anxiety, a symptom, a stand taken

#### Bowen

- <a id="F31"></a>**F31** “The "I position" defines principle and action in terms of, "This is what I think, or believe" and, "This is what I will do or will not do," without impinging one's own values or beliefs on others.” Bowen, FTiCP ch21 L117. The wording Bowen gives, with its condition: without imposing on others. Whether taking such a stand is a functioning shift is open (status item 15).
- <a id="F32"></a>**F32** “And to be a self in an emotional system means you got to believe in it enough to take an action stand. A self is never defined in words. It's defined only when you can take an action stand. When self can take an action stand that's not punitive.” Bowen, BS4 L685. Look for the action, not the statement. "Not punitive" is part of it.

#### Kerr

- <a id="F33"></a>**F33** “This process of change has been called "defining a self" because visible action is taken to which others respond. A change in basic level can be achieved while in relationship to emotionally significant others, but not when others are avoided or when one's actions disrupt a relationship.” Kerr, FE4 L104. Defining a self is the route to a change in basic level. Whether a coder records it as a functioning shift, a relationship move or a third kind is open (status item 15).
- <a id="F34"></a>**F34** “When someone attempts to be more of a self in a relationship system, the absolutely predictable response from important others is, "You are wrong; change back; if you don't, these are the consequences!" In fact, if such responses do not occur, one's efforts to define more of a self are probably inconsequential.” Kerr, FE4 L106. Kerr's test for a real step toward self: others react. OPEN_QUESTIONS 13 asks whether that reaction must be seen before the code.
- <a id="F35"></a>**F35** “More importantly, it is not fueled by anger. Anger can sometimes be a stimulus to clarify one's thinking, but it is not a reliable guide for action. When someone angrily and dogmatically claims to be a "self," he is usually unsure of his position and is blaming others for his plight in life.” Kerr, FE4 L108. An angry claim to be a self is not evidence of a shift up.
- <a id="F36"></a>**F36** “The father may have drunk heavily over the years, but he may have also maintained a fairly adequate level of functioning in many areas of his life.” Kerr, FE10 L37. Heavy drinking and adequate functioning can coexist. A symptom does not by itself show a functioning shift.

#### Havstad

- <a id="F37"></a>**F37** “Symptom change in a family member is observed as shifts in their emotional functioning that follow from shifts in the family emotional system. The symptom change is seen and documented as part of the family unit shift.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L68. Here a symptom change and a functioning shift are one observation. R-0424 keeps them apart (status item 3).

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="F39"></a>**F39** “Anxiety as defined here is one category of emotion. *In a nutshell, as anxiety goes up, complex thinking goes down.* A function of anxiety is to inhibit thinking so as to fall back on an older, simpler script.” Patrick, ANX L93. Thinking going down is here a sign of anxiety up. The white paper's functioning definition (F7) names the same thinking, so the two codes need separate evidence (status item 9).


### 2e. Reciprocal functioning: one up, another down

#### Bowen

- <a id="F41"></a>**F41** “It is factual that dysfunctioning and overfunctioning exist together. On one level this is a smooth working, flexible, reciprocating mechanism in which one member automatically overfunctions to compensate for the dysfunction of the other who is temporarily ill.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35. Bowen calls the pairing factual. The flexible form, one covering for another who is ill, is ordinary.
- <a id="F42"></a>**F42** “the "helpless one" becoming more helpless in response to the overfunctioning of the other. ... It is far easier for the overfunctioning one to "tone down" the overfunctioning than for the poorly functioning one to "pull up."” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L41. A shift up in one can bring a shift down in the other, during treatment too.
- <a id="F43"></a>**F43** “The occasional brief shifts are striking. One of the best examples of this is that of the regressed schizophrenic person who pulls up to resourceful functioning when his parents are sick, only to fall back when they have recovered.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L65. A shift up in the weakest member can follow others' illness and reverse when they recover.
- <a id="F44"></a>**F44** “One of the selfs in the common self becomes dominant and the other submissive or adaptive. Said in another way, the dominant one gains a higher level of functional self and appears "stronger," at the expense of the adaptive one who gave up self and who is functionally "weaker."” Bowen, FTiCP ch21 L25. The dominant one "appears" stronger; Bowen puts "stronger" and "weaker" in quotation marks. "Adaptive" here means the one who gives up self (status item 14).
- <a id="F45"></a>**F45** “Had he yielded to her demand, or attacked, he would have slipped back to her level. When he held his position, her emotional reaction represented a pull-up to his level.” Bowen, FTiCP ch21 L121. In Bowen's example, one person holding a position is followed by the other's rise.

#### Kerr

- <a id="F46"></a>**F46** “An "overfunctioning" person shapes the attitudes, feelings, and behavior of an "underfunctioning" person as much as the underfunctioning one shapes the attitudes, feelings, and behavior of the overfunctioning one.” Kerr, FE2 L216. Neither side is the cause. Both positions are held up from both sides.
- <a id="F47"></a>**F47** “An underfunctioning person who has been chronically ill many improve his functioning and experience a dramatic remission of symptoms if his overfunctioning partner becomes ill or dies.” Kerr, FE2 L220. "many" [sic] for "may". A shift up can follow the partner's decline.
- <a id="F48"></a>**F48** “two people with basic levels of 35 who marry might, during the course of the marriage, do enough "borrowing" and "trading" of "selves" that one spouse's functional level rises to an average of 55 and the other's drops to an average of 15 . This borrowing and trading process relates to the ways in which people adapt to one another to relieve anxiety.” Kerr, FE4 L58. One spouse's rise is paid for by the other's fall. The rise is a functioning shift up, not a gain in solid self. The numbers illustrate; SARF records no levels (status item 1).
- <a id="F49"></a>**F49** “The lower the basic level, the more the functional level will fluctuate widely and the more there will be significant discrepancies between the functional levels of closely involved people (seesaw effect).” Kerr, FE4 L60. The seesaw: large gaps between closely involved people.
- <a id="F50"></a>**F50** “While a dominant-subordinate or overfunctioning-underfunctioning reciprocity in a relationship is an important mechanism for binding anxiety and stabilizing the functioning of both people, an increase in the levels of chronic anxiety can exaggerate this pattern to the point that one person's functioning is so impaired that symptoms develop.” Kerr, FE7 L51. The reciprocity stabilizes both people until anxiety exaggerates it.
- <a id="F51"></a>**F51** “The one making the most adjustments may be an overfunctioning person who, feeling an exaggerated sense of responsibility about making things "right" for others, is trying to do too much, or it may be an underfunctioning person who, feeling little confidence in his ability to make decisions, is depending on others too much.” Kerr, FE7 L51. Either position can be the one that adapts most. Kerr's "adaptive one" is open (status item 14).
- <a id="F52"></a>**F52** “As chronic anxiety increases, the one inclined to overfunction becomes "stronger" and more dominant, and the one inclined to underfunction becomes "weaker" and more subordinate. Both spouses exert about equal pressure on one another to assume certain emotional postures.” Kerr, FE7 L85. Rising anxiety moves one up and one down, from both sides.

### 2f. Order and timing: claims, never defaults

Status item 11 applies to every line here: record the order of the dates, and keep any direction as a hypothesis.

#### Bowen

- <a id="F53"></a>**F53** “in the chronic states there is evidence that the dysfunction appears later to compensate for overfunction in the other. However it develops, the overfunction-dysfunction is a reciprocating mechanism.” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L35. Bowen allows either order. Record the dates (status item 11).
- <a id="F54"></a>**F54** “It is not possible to do day to day or week to week evaluations of scale levels because of the wide shifts in the functional level of pseudo-self in low-scale people. A compliment can raise the functioning level of self and criticism can lower it.” Bowen, FTiCP ch21 L35. Why SARF records shifts, not levels (status item 1): week-to-week movement is functional.
- <a id="F55"></a>**F55** “This pattern is so predictable that absence of an emotional reaction is good evidence that the differentiating effort was not successful. ... If the differentiating one can stay on course without defending self or counterattacking, the emotional reaction is usually brief and the other then expresses appreciation.” Bowen, FTiCP ch21 L119. Bowen's order after a step toward self: a reaction, a brief upheaval, then calm. A claim, not a default.
- <a id="F56"></a>**F56** “The more one spouse under-functions, the more the other sort of over-functions or makes up for the dysfunction. So as anxiety episodes go along, one spouse dysfunctions more and the other functions better. Until this one is so dysfunctional that they develop illness.” Bowen, BS5 L186. Bowen's sequence: opposite shifts in two spouses over episodes, then illness. A claim, not a default.

#### Kerr

- <a id="F57"></a>**F57** “After a divorce, the functioning of one spouse may rise and that of the other may decline. This is a change in functional level, not in basic level. The functional level of a person with a low basic level can rise and fall many times even during just a few hours. Functional level may be higher at work than it is at home.” Kerr, FE4 L64. Shifts can take hours. A divorce can move two people in opposite directions. Functioning can differ between work and home.
- <a id="F58"></a>**F58** “A parent's functional level may either increase or decrease after the birth of a child. It may drop for a long period following the death of a parent.” Kerr, FE4 L64. An event does not fix the direction of a functioning shift.
- <a id="F59"></a>**F59** “Such a stance in a family, while it creates a transient period of disharmony, can result in other family members' beginning to function on the basis of a more thoughtful and self-determined direction of their own.” Kerr, FE4 L124. One person's stance can be followed by others' shifts, after a period of disharmony.
- <a id="F60"></a>**F60** “The "helpfulness" of the overfunctioning one may relieve immediate distress for the dysfunctional one, but it solidifies the dysfunctional one's helpless and dependent position.” Kerr, FE7 L81. Short-term relief can hold a functioning position in place.

#### Havstad

- <a id="F62"></a>**F62** “Additionally, it was observed that shifts in the subjects' functional level of self, along with changes in their anxiety level, often followed the shift in the family system that preceded their diet to goal weight. The variable of shift in the functioning level of subjects was added as the fourth variable to be documented.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L54. "Often", not always. Functioning was added as the fourth variable during this analysis.

### 4e. A son who began to function when his mother collapsed (published case, FE ch10)

#### Questions

The mother's "depression and heavy drinking" interferes with conduct, so it passes the symptom test (status item 3). Whether the same fact also carries a functioning-down code for her is not ruled. ### 4f. A wife "surer of herself" after her husband's mentor died (Havstad's pilot study, published)

- <a id="F112"></a>**F112** “The husband became less sure of himself, and his wife became surer of herself in relation to him. The shift in the couple has no date; the start of the diet is dated to the death.
- <a id="F113"></a>**F113** “Three women dieted following shifts in their marriages in which the wife's reciprocal position with respect to her husband improved as his became more adverse and the wife's anxiety decreased.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L43. The group this case belongs to.

#### Questions

Are "less sure of himself" and "surer of herself" two functioning shifts, one on each spouse, or one relationship move described from both sides? The IRR coders treated "unsure of oneself" as functioning down (status item 17); that is not a ruling. Is starting a diet a symptom-down shift, a functioning-up shift, or neither (F29)? Weight passes the symptom test only if it interferes (status item 3), and the article gives no interference. "The wife's anxiety decreased" has no evidence of its own, so it is not recorded as a fact. The couple's shift is undated, so its place before or after the diet cannot be recorded, and no default order fills the gap.
