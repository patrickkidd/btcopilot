# Distance (D)

For coders: what the original authors wrote about emotional distance, where to find it, and what is decided. Read it for food for thought while coding. It is not a rule book; section 1 says which rules exist.

- Built 2026-09-26 from the sources listed in [`README.md`](README.md). Every quote below was checked word for word against its source file by the quote checker; the check is repeatable.
- This is the public edition: excerpts from confidential sources are left out, and so are the paths to the source files ([`README.md`](README.md)).
- Ids (D1, D2, ...) are stable. Location keys (FE3 L136, IRR M2 L2056, ...) are explained in [`README.md`](README.md).
- Transcripts of recordings are machine transcriptions. A word marked [sic] is as transcribed.
- Related pages: [`cutoff.md`](cutoff.md) holds the passages on cutoff, including Kerr's "Cutoff and distance mean the same thing". [`conflict.md`](conflict.md) holds the silent-standoff dispute. [`anxiety.md`](anxiety.md) status item 6 says why a distance code never implies anxiety up.

## Coder summary

Distance is in the white paper's list of relationship moves, but almost nothing about coding it is ruled. What is ruled: only shifts are recorded (status item 1), and a distance code never implies an anxiety shift (status item 4). Whether "away", "distance" and "cutoff" are one kind of move or several is open for the IRR group (status item 3). The coders agreed, without a ruling, that a geographic move is coded "moved", not distance (status item 7). Whether work, a divorce or not feeling "close" counts as distance is open (status items 8 and 9).

The passages that matter most:
- D6, D8: Kerr, distance "is a feature of all relationships", so it is not a separate category of dysfunction.
- D7: distance can be physical avoidance or internal withdrawal, and it shows as avoidance of each other and of upsetting subjects.
- D16: "The urge to avoid others is a function of a lack of emotional separation." Distance is not independence.
- D9, D25: distance is neither good nor bad; it can lower anxiety in the distancer and raise it in the other.
- D40, D44, D45: the coders' own words on away, distance and moved.

The main off-theory trap: reading geography, a busy job or a quiet person as emotional distance. A second trap: reading distance as healthy independence or as growth (D16, D20).

## 1. Status of the code

### Patrick (writing and rulings)

1. **Record a shift, never a level.** RULED. Ruling R-0054 (records shifts, not levels) — see the private edition. A pair that is "always distant" goes in once as a baseline (CODING section 8).
2. **Distance is one kind in the value space; relationship moves are automatic.** Patrick's white paper, his writing and not a ruling ([`conflict.md`](conflict.md) C8, C9). The list names distance and cutoff separately.
3. **Away, distance, cutoff: one kind of move or several?** OPEN, for the IRR group. Ruling R-0439 (coding judgment calls are left to the IRR group) — see the private edition. Patrick's 2021 writing made distance "synonymous with away" (D12); in the IRR meetings he said "Away has a little less assumption" (D40). Distance against cutoff is [`cutoff.md`](cutoff.md) status item 4. Whether toward and away mean contact or desirability is OPEN_QUESTIONS 14.
4. **A distance code does not imply an anxiety shift.** RULED 2026-09-26 ([`anxiety.md`](anxiety.md) status item 6, where the coders' argument is A102 and A104). Kerr's "anxiety disappears" with distance (D7) is theory, not a coding rule.
5. **A reading from theory stays apart from the facts.** RULED 2026-09-26. Ruling OPEN_QUESTIONS 5 (facts and theory-based readings are kept in separate layers) — see the private edition. So "the father distanced because the mother focused on the baby" is kept, if at all, as a labelled hypothesis (D53, D54).
6. **A couple's split as away moves: undecided.** R-0057 said a couple's split over having children Ruling R-0057 (a worked coding example, marked undecided) — see the private edition. R-0511 (2026-09-25) marked it undecided, with the words "undecided about R-0057".

### Coders' group (IRR)

7. **A geographic move is "moved", not distance.** Agreed by the group at IRR meeting 2, without a Patrick ruling (reading notes irr section 6). The argument is D44 to D46; Patrick still called it "a deep question" whether distance is "baked in" with a divorce (D46).
8. **Distance or reality?** OPEN. Leaving for work, taking a call, living far away: Havstad did not know "if it's distance or reality" (D48); Patrick, "I had to go to work, so what are you saying I'm emotionally distant?" (D49). Havstad's principle: "The mechanisms are driven by anxiety, not by reality" (D50). No test for telling them apart is ruled.
9. **"Not close" and "stepping back".** Discussed, not ruled. Not calling oneself close is not evidence of distance (D51). "Stepping back" can be objectivity rather than distance (D52).
10. **Silent standoff: conflict or distance?** OPEN; see [`conflict.md`](conflict.md) status item 10.

## 2. What the original authors wrote

### 2a. What distance is

#### Bowen

- <a id="D69"></a>**D69** “This emotional distance, which I have called the emotional divorce, helps prevent the loss of identity and conflict that comes with closeness.” Bowen, FTiCP ch6 L11. The early name for marital distance, with its claimed function.
- <a id="D4"></a>**D4** “Both can function relatively well as long as they keep relationships distant or casual. ... The most common mechanism is the use of sufficient emotional distance for each to function with a reasonable level of "pseudo-self."” Bowen, FTiCP ch9 L67.
- <a id="D1"></a>**D1** “The most universal mechanism is emotional distance from each other. It is present in all marriages to some degree, and in a high percentage of marriages to a major degree.” Bowen, FTiCP ch16 L165. The passage Patrick read out in IRR meeting 4 (D55).
- <a id="D3"></a>**D3** “this is so general I haven't included it in the three major ways of dealing with it in a nuclear family but the first is they get the distance from each other. In other words by keeping the distance they can each keep more of a self.” Bowen, BS2 L1101. Distance set apart from the three "major ways" as too general.
- <a id="D2"></a>**D2** “The emotional distance helps them to control the intensity or the discomfort of the fusion.” Bowen, BS5 L38. In this lecture distance is the first of four patterns, not set apart.
- <a id="D5"></a>**D5** “It's distance between the spouses, marital conflict, and each one of these things absorbs amounts of this anxiety.” Bowen, BKIS-04-pt1 @00:03:54.

#### Kerr

- <a id="D6"></a>**D6** “One response to the pressure to adapt, however, is a feature of all relationships. This response is emotional distance. Anxiety generated by the relationship dilemma is absorbed or bound through people enforcing some degree of distance.” Kerr, FE3 L134.
- <a id="D7"></a>**D7** “Emotional distance provides some "emotional insulation" from people's impact on one another. It can be achieved by physically avoiding others and by various forms of internally withdrawing.” Kerr, FE3 L136. Physical or internal; the same paragraph's test of forced contact is [`anxiety.md`](anxiety.md) A39.
- <a id="D8"></a>**D8** “Emotional distance is not considered a distinct category of dysfunction because it is a feature of all relationships and it is intertwined with all the patterns of emotional functioning in a nuclear family.” Kerr, FE7 L29. Kerr's three categories leave distance out; Bowen's four-pattern lecture puts it in (D2).
- <a id="D10"></a>**D10** “If an individual cannot control his reactions to another person, and if the reactions are intense, he must distance from that person to control himself.” Kerr, FE7 L289 (footnote 10).

#### Havstad

- <a id="D11"></a>**D11** “Nineteen dieted to goal weight following shifts in active triangles in their nuclear or extended families in which they increased emotional distance from one family member as they increased closeness with another.” Havstad and Sheffield, ART L43. Distance from one person paired with closeness to another.

#### Patrick (writing and rulings)

- <a id="D12"></a>**D12** “When tension between two individuals increases beyond a comfortable threshold, one or both will attempt to return below the threshold. There are four ways to accomplish this. Distance is synonymous with away.” Patrick, EEM L21 (2021). Compare his 2026 words in D40.
- <a id="D13"></a>**D13** “An amicable resignation from a board position is several simultaneous away moves from the resigner to each of the other board members ... So is avoiding a Christmas holiday. So is spending a few days apart from someone because you had an argument with them.” Patrick, LOG L21 (2021). Examples of away; whether each is distance is status item 3.
- <a id="D14"></a>**D14** “A person in a dyad has a limited number of behavioral pathways when they exceed their threshold for tension. ... They can 1) distance, 2) argue/fight, 3) dominate+submit, 4) pull in a third.” Patrick, TDTA L44 (2021).

### 2b. How distance shows, and what is not distance

#### Bowen

- <a id="D68"></a>**D68** “The marriages had the form and content of closeness in that they went through the actions of closeness and used terms of endearment associated with closeness, but emotion was obliterated.” Bowen, FTiCP ch5 L27. Distance can look like an ideal marriage. The couple's description is a statement, not the code.
- <a id="D20"></a>**D20** “One of the biggest problems in people thinking about the concept of differentiation is that they consider distance to be differentiation or they can consider emotional fusion to be emotional closeness.” Bowen, BS5 L352.
- <a id="D21"></a>**D21** “it's common for this person who is away from it to say that they have handled things with the family and they're out of it, which is not differentiation. That's distancing or going away from it.” Bowen, BS4 L94. "I'm out of it" is a candidate sign of distance, not of growth.
- <a id="D22"></a>**D22** “Nobody ever handles an emotional system by running away from it. And this is what people do.” Bowen, BS2 L674.

#### Kerr

- <a id="D15"></a>**D15** “With distancing, anxiety "disappears" and is manifested in people's avoidance of one another and of potentially upsetting subjects.” Kerr, FE3 L136. Two things a record can show: avoiding the person, avoiding the subject.
- <a id="D16"></a>**D16** “The phrase "emotional separation" is not to be equated with people avoiding each other. The urge to avoid others is a function of a lack of emotional separation.” Kerr, FE3 L188 (footnote 6).
- <a id="D18"></a>**D18** “The more people use emotional distance to reduce anxiety in their relationship, for example, the more likely it is that one or both people will invest energy in another relationship or project that has emotional significance.” Kerr, FE3 L200 (footnote 22). A new investment elsewhere is a claimed companion of distance, not proof of it.
- <a id="D17"></a>**D17** “People who never felt "close" to their parents are usually people who have managed an intense attachment to the family with distance and denial.” Kerr, FE7 L217. "Never close" describes a baseline, not a dated move.

### 2c. Distance is neither good nor bad

#### Bowen

- <a id="D24"></a>**D24** “Keeping in mind that keeping distance from each other is one of the better ways. I list the three ways they deal with it because these are the three things that end up with symptoms.” Bowen, BS2 L1328.

#### Kerr

- <a id="D26"></a>**D26** “While distance can provide some stability to a relationship, it is a compromise. Comfort achieved by emotional closeness is balanced against comfort achieved by emotional distance.” Kerr, FE3 L136.
- <a id="D25"></a>**D25** “In addition, when people use distance or denial to manage anxiety, they may lower it in themselves, but raise it in others. One may achieve more comfort for oneself, but increase the anxiety in those to whom one is emotionally connected.” Kerr, FE5 L99.
- <a id="D9"></a>**D9** “Emotional distance, whether maintained by physical avoidance or by some form of internal withdrawal, is not "good" or "bad."” Kerr, FE7 L289 (footnote 10).

### 2d. Distance with closeness, pursuit and a third person

#### Bowen

- <a id="D32"></a>**D32** “If I hear you, you're saying that a lot of the distance with father is related to the intensity with mother.” Kerr asking Bowen, BKIS-06-pt2a @00:13:40. A distance in one relationship tied to intensity in another: a triangle reading.

#### Kerr

- <a id="D27"></a>**D27** “A common anxiety-driven cycle is emotional neediness in one person triggering distance in another, which triggers more neediness in the first, which triggers more distance in the other.” Kerr, FE5 L81. Pursuit and distance as a cycle; both needs rise together ([`anxiety.md`](anxiety.md) A40).
- <a id="D29"></a>**D29** “The emotional support this third person provides for the uncomfortable one (support based on emotional fusion) can create more distance in the original twosome than would have been possible without a triangle.” Kerr, FE6 L75.

### 2e. Order and timing: claims, never defaults

[`anxiety.md`](anxiety.md) status item 8 applies here too: record the order of the dates, and keep any direction as a hypothesis.

#### Kerr

- <a id="D33"></a>**D33** “The husband may get reactive to his wife's neediness and become critical of her and pull away. His distancing isolates the wife, which further increases her anxiety and yearning for support.” Kerr, FE5 L17. A pregnancy, then criticism and pulling away. "May": an example, not a rule.

## 4. Worked examples

Each example gives the facts as said, then the questions a coder faces. The questions are left open where the rulings leave them open.

### 4a. Sarah's brother "has really taken a step back" (IRR synthetic case)

Sarah is a synthetic persona written by an LLM, not a client (case card irr-synthetic-sarah-arthur).

#### Coders' group (IRR)

- <a id="D60"></a>**D60** “Michael has always been a bit more laid back, I guess, but I feel like since mom's diagnosis, he's really taken a step back. I mean, I don't want to blame him completely. Maybe he just doesn't know how to handle it.” Sarah's statement as read out, IRR M1 L321.

#### Questions

The four coders gave away, distance, distance plus overfunctioning, and overfunctioning (reading notes irr section 4). Patrick later dropped his away (D47). Is "taken a step back" distance, or could it be objectivity (D52)? From whom: Sarah, their mother, or both? "Always a bit more laid back" is a baseline; "since mom's diagnosis" dates a change. Does distance fold into underfunctioning (Guillermo's question), now that the rulings on over- and underfunctioning are rescinded and those codes undecided (R-0510)?

### 4b. Sarah's father "in Florida with his girlfriend" (IRR synthetic case)

#### Coders' group (IRR)

- <a id="D61"></a>**D61** “It's like Michael's just avoiding it all. As for my parents, well, Dad's in Florida with his girlfriend and.” Sarah's statement as read out, IRR M2 L1387.

#### Questions

The group settled on "moved" for the father (status item 7). Nothing in the statement dates the move or says anything about contact. "Avoiding it all" is Sarah's word for her brother: a report of a move, or her reading of it?

### 4c. The wife, her mother, and the drinking (Kerr, FE ch6)

A published illustration, not one family's record.

#### Kerr

- <a id="D62"></a>**D62** “Her distress eventually becomes so great, however, that it overrides her more automatic urge to avoid conflict and she attempts to talk to her husband about her unhappiness. By the time she attempts to talk about it, she and her husband are both so upset and reactive that the discussion disintegrates. Unproductive exchanges such as these gradually incline both people to avoid bringing up "unpleasant" subjects.” Kerr, FE6 L77.
- <a id="D63"></a>**D63** “Mother's anger at her daughter's husband supports the daughter's use of emotional distance to deal with the problem and accentuates her distancing behavior.” Kerr, FE6 L79.
- <a id="D64"></a>**D64** “the husband, previously calmed by a certain amount of marital distance, becomes quite reactive to what is now experienced as "excessive" distance. ... He comes across so needy and demanding, however, that it prompts his wife to distance further.” Kerr, FE6 L81.
- <a id="D65"></a>**D65** “Unsuccessful in his attempts to restore a comfortable level of togetherness, the husband starts drinking more. A certain amount of drinking can relieve some of these relationship dilemmas and provide stability.” Kerr, FE6 L81. Kerr does not call the drinking distance.

#### Questions

The sequence as Kerr tells it: a failed talk; both avoid "unpleasant" subjects; the wife talks to her mother; her distance grows; the husband pursues; she distances further; he drinks more. Which of these are distance moves, by whom, from whom? Is the wife's talking to her mother a toward move, a triangle move, or both? Is the drinking a symptom (it passes the interference test only once it "begins to impair the husband's functioning"), a move, or neither? The order is Kerr's example, not observed dates.

### 4d. Retirement takes away the distance (Kerr, FE ch8)

#### Kerr

- <a id="D66"></a>**D66** “Over the years, the marriage had required considerable emotional distance to keep it in equilibrium ... Husband and wife were physically together much more than in the past and that physical proximity inundated the marriage with "too much togetherness." The wife felt more cut off from her husband and less able to control her own life when he was physically present than when he was away at work.” Kerr, FE8 L57.
- <a id="D67"></a>**D67** “Anxiety that was bound previously in emotional distance "spilled over" into clinical symptoms. The wife began to drink heavily and developed a duodenal ulcer.” Kerr, FE8 L57.

#### Questions

The retirement is a dated event. The wife felt "more cut off" while physically closer: physical closeness and emotional distance at once. Is anything here a distance move, or is it a loss of distance (a toward move forced by the event)? The symptoms are dated after the retirement; "spilled over" is Kerr's reading of the link, kept as a hypothesis (status item 5).
