## Before you start

Mac, open `https://127.0.0.1:8891/personal/` instead — the name turin only resolves over the

**The conversation every walk turns on** is Marcus's move to Arizona, on the Whitlock record. The
three coders on it are Lena Whitaker, Tomas Reyes and Priya Nandakumar. They have all finished,
and they disagree about seven moments, about who Corinne was born to, and about whether Marcus

**The order matters.** Walks 1 to 6 are one chain: each leaves the sandbox in the state the next
one needs. Walk 3 opens the vote that Walk 4 casts, Walk 4 fills the meeting that Walk 5 runs, and
Walk 5 produces the screen Walk 6 reads. Walks 7, 8 and 9 stand on their own and can be done at
any point.

**One thing that will catch you out.** The button that opens the sessions sheet, the small glyph at
the bottom left of the chat, only exists on the chat screen. On the task card, the agenda, the
ballot and the meeting it is not there. The arrow at the top left walks you back to the chat, where
the button is again. Expect to use that arrow a lot.

**Two things are known to be wrong before you look.**

- A person's row in the people list shows a second faint line under the name. The written spec says
  a name and nothing else. Nobody has decided which is right; you are the one who decides.
- When a coder writes a sentence about a marriage, the line the app writes back says the month and
  the year — "married · Jun 1970" — where the spec says the year alone. The birth line is exactly
  as written.

**Putting a walk back.** To run the chain again from the start, paste these three lines into a
terminal. They put the three coders back as finished, clear every vote, and close the vote again.


## Walk 1 — a coder's one task

Open Lena's link. You land on the chat, on her own record.

1. Tap the sessions button at the bottom left. At the foot of the sheet is a button naming her
   one task.
2. Tap it. You get a card: the conversation it is about, where it is frozen, and how long it
   should take. Under the card is a faint list of what she has already finished.
3. The card's button reads "vote" and is grey and will not press. That is right: the vote has not
   been opened yet, so there is nothing for her to do. Walk 3 opens it.
4. Tap the arrow at the top left to go back to the chat.

What to look for: whether the card reads as one thing to do, never a list to choose from.

## Walk 2 — coding a conversation

Sign in as yourself, with your passkey, in your own window. You are one of the coders on this
conversation and have not finished.

1. Tap the sessions button, then the task button at the foot of the sheet, then the button on the
   card, which reads "start". The conversation opens as a thread you cannot type into.
2. A faint hairline marks the last point already agreed. Everything under it is what this task is
   about. The bottom is marked with the point the conversation is frozen at; turns after it are
   not shown.
3. Tap a line. It is outlined in green.
4. Type, in your own words, what that line tells you happened, and send. After a few seconds a
   line appears under your words saying what was written into the record. Your own words stay in
   the thread above it.
5. Now type a sentence about the family's shape rather than an event: "Marcus and Delphine married
   in 1970." The line it writes back names both of them, says married, and gives the date. This is
   the line that says the month where the spec says the year alone.
6. Type one more: "Corinne is the daughter of Marcus and Delphine." The line it writes back reads
   "Corinne · daughter of Marcus & Delphine". Nothing you typed named a birth event, and no birth
   event is invented; what is added is who her parents are.
7. **Do not tap Done.** Done submits your coding and it cannot be changed after that, and it would
   add a fourth coder to the vote the next four walks depend on.

What to look for: whether writing a sentence about who somebody's parents are feels like the same
act as writing a sentence about something that happened.

## Walk 3 — the agenda, and opening the vote

Open Ruth's link in a second private window. She lands on the chat.

1. Tap the sessions button. The last button in the sheet reads "Next meeting" with the date. Tap
   it.
2. The screen shows the meeting date, then what is on the agenda, then a heading for the coders and
   every coder with what they are doing: not started, coding, done or voted. Lena, Tomas and Priya
   are there. The coach is not, because it never votes.
3. Under the coders is a line saying how many are closed out, and a button offering to nudge the
   ones who are not. **Do not tap the nudge** — the sandbox sends real mail, and the fixture coders'
   addresses are not real. Read the words and move on.
4. Below that is "open the vote". Tap it. The button is replaced by a plain line saying the vote is
   open.
5. A new button appears offering to run the meeting. Leave it; that is Walk 5.
6. At the foot is the next meeting's agenda, which fills itself from flagged rules alone. A moment
   the room left unresolved is not listed: it stays unresolved as data and never comes back to a
   later meeting. Nobody who has not finished is listed either, because the coder list above
   already says who is not done.

What to look for: whether everything you have to do as the person running this is on this one
screen. If you find yourself wanting a second screen, say so.

## Walk 4 — Lena's ballot

Go back to Lena's window and reload.

1. The card's button is live now and reads "vote". Tap it.
2. Before the moments come the people and the couples, because a moment about somebody nobody has
   agreed on yet cannot be settled. The first of those is Corinne: two coders had her born to
   Marcus and Delphine, one had her born to nobody on the record. Each version is drawn as a small
   family with her in the middle, so you see the argument as a shape before you read a word.
3. Further on is the couple Marcus and Delphine: two coders had them married, one had them together
   and not married.
4. On one of those, tap "change". The person's own editor opens over the ballot — the same editor
   the record is corrected in — already filled in. Saving it would join the count as one more
   version. Close it without saving.
5. Then come the moments, one per screen: the date, who it happened to, what happened, and the line
   from the transcript it came from.
6. Under each one are the opinions the coders wrote. They carry no names and no counts; who wrote
   what is kept for the meeting. Where a coder left the moment out entirely, a faint line says so
   in plain words, with nothing to tap, because leaving something out is not a vote.
7. Above the moment is a row of dots, one per moment, with the one you are on enlarged and green.
8. Tap an opinion. It records your vote and moves you on.
9. On the next one, tap "change". The event editor opens over the ballot, prefilled, so you can
   write an opinion nobody wrote. Close it without saving.
10. Under the change and drop buttons is a line asking why you voted as you did. It is optional and
    you can leave it empty.
11. Tap "open in transcript". You are put into the conversation at the line in question and cannot
    type into it. The arrow at the top left brings you back to the item you left.
12. Tap "drop" on one. That is a vote that it should not be in the record at all.
13. Tap "next" to skip one. It should still be waiting if you go round again.
14. Work to the end, so the ballot has something for every item.

What to look for: whether the drawing tells you what the disagreement is; whether you can decide
most of this on your own; and whether anything makes you want to know who wrote an opinion.

## Walk 5 — Ruth's meeting

In Ruth's window, on the agenda screen, tap the button that runs the meeting.

1. At the top, one title line: the word Meeting, the day, and the conversation. Under it one line
   of three figures, each labelled: how many moments, how many disputed, and what agreement was
   before the vote. Under that the three colours with a word each. Nothing on this screen says a
   figure twice.
2. Under that is a row of dots, one per moment, teal where the vote agreed and amber where it did
   not, with a small count beside a disputed one. Tap any dot: it turns green and the list jumps to
   that moment's card.
3. Under the dots is the order the list is read in: by how split things are, or by time. By
   divergence the most split come first and the agreed ones follow in their own section. By time
   everything is in one list in the order it happened, the agreed ones marked agreed. Try both.
4. Every row names who and what. A moment with no date says it has no date yet. No row reads as
   "an item".
5. The people and the couples are at the top of the list, before the moments, each version with its
   own "keep this" and its own drawing. They are not on the row of dots: the dots stay one per
   moment, and the line of colours ends with how many people and couples the record holds.
6. On a disputed card, each version has its own "keep this" beside the names behind it, so keeping
   one is one tap. Here, and only here, the opinions carry names and counts. Every coder shows the
   same way, as initials with full stops. Nobody shows as an email address. Below the versions are
   "change" and "mark unresolved".
7. Settle Corinne's parents and the couple by tapping "keep this" on the version you want.
8. Tap a row the vote agreed on. The same card opens, with a close button at its top right.
   "Keep this" is greyed out once the room has kept it; change and mark unresolved stay live.
9. Give a choice to the first open moment and watch the button at the bottom: it counts down how
   many still need one.
10. Work through the rest. When the last one has a choice, the ratify button comes alive and reads
    just "ratify".
11. Tap ratify. The button goes dead, reads "ratifying" and shows three dots. It takes ten to
    twenty seconds, which is the coach writing the guideline changes and scoring its own reading.
    A second tap does nothing. Wait for the result screen.

What to look for: whether the room could actually be run off this screen, and whether the order the
items come in is the order you would want to take them.

## Walk 6 — what the meeting produced

Ratifying puts you straight onto the result screen.

1. At the top: how many moments were ratified, how many were left unresolved, one count saying how
   many people and couples the room ratified and how many it left open, agreement on the first pass
   and after ratification side by side, and how the coach's own reading scored against the agreed
   record.
2. Then the guideline changes the AI wrote out of what the room decided. Each one shows the item it
   came from and the margin it was decided by, and carries a link to flag it for the next meeting.
   Tap one flag and watch it change to say it is flagged.
3. Then where the AI's reading differed from the room, with its reason, as an audit and not a vote.
4. Then what each coder tends to do differently from the others.
5. Go back to Lena's window and reload. Her card now says nothing is on the agenda to code yet, and
   under "done before" is the conversation just ratified. Tap that row and this result screen opens
   again.

## Walk 7 — a person's parents and partners

Any window will do. Tap the list button at the right-hand end of the row under the picture, then
the people tab.

1. Tap someone. Under the family heading the editor asks who they were born to: the couples on the
   record, and nobody. The couple that person is themselves part of is not offered.
2. Somebody with nobody on the record is also offered "add parents". Tap it. Two people appear,
   named after the child — "Corinne's father" and "Corinne's mother" — and the person is now born to
   that couple. The offer is gone, because a person is born to one couple; to change it you pick
   another.
3. Under that is one row for every other person they have ever been bonded to, and a row to add
   one. A row says the other person's name, whether they married, and the year it ended when the
   record has one. Tap a row: that bond's own small editor opens in place, asking who the other
   person is and whether they married. When it started and when it ended are moments, so they are
   said to the coach and not typed here.
4. Look at the people list itself. Each row shows a name and a second faint line under it. This is
   the known difference from the spec, which says the name alone. You decide which is right.

What to look for: whether a wrong link — somebody married to their own mother, say — is something
you can see and undo here.

## Walk 8 — a professional's screens

Open Nadine's link in a third private window. She lands on the chat, with no coding task card and
no coding task in the sessions sheet: a professional licence is not a coding role.

1. Tap the sessions button. The sheet has a button to start a new session and, beside it, one to
   upload a recording and one to start a note. Tap upload: the sheet gives way to the upload sheet
   rather than stacking on top of it. Back out.
2. Tap the new note button. A note starts as a session of its own and is listed with the sessions,
   labelled as a note.
3. Tap outside the sheet to put it away, then tap your account mark at the top right.
4. The list of records is headed Cases. A tick marks the one the app is on. She has six: Ortega,
   Lindqvist, Bergstrom, Marchetti and two named Whitlock. Tap another one. The chat title row
   should name the case you switched to, and the sessions should be that case's.
5. Now make the window about 1280 wide. The events and people drawer should pin open on the right
   of the chat instead of sliding over it. Everything else should be the same screen. On the wide
   screen the list button focuses the search box rather than opening the drawer, because the drawer
   is already open.

What to look for: two cases with the same name and nothing to tell them apart is worth a decision.

## Walk 9 — the command line

There is no admin web page. These run in a terminal. Each prints a table; adding `--json` to any of
them gives the same rows as JSON.

Set the terminal up once:

```
source /Users/patrick/worktrees/fd362-sandbox/env.sh 8890 /Users/patrick/worktrees/fd362-sandbox/beta3.db
cd /Users/patrick/theapp
```

Then type these three.

1. `uv run flask admin review agenda` — one row per conversation on the agenda, with columns for
   the meeting date, whether coding has started, and whether the vote is open. Right now it is the
   one row for Marcus's move to Arizona.
2. `uv run flask admin users list` — one row per account, with the email, the name, the roles,
   whether it is active, whether it has a professional licence, and how many records it owns.
   Thirteen rows: yours and the twelve fixture accounts.
3. `uv run flask admin licences plans` — one row per plan, with its code, its name, the amount, the
   interval and whether it is active. One row: Professional Monthly at 19.99 a month.

What to look for: whether a table is the right shape for the answer, and what you would want to
type next that is not here.
