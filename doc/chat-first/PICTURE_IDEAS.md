# Round 7 — picture spot concepts (2026-09-23)

A record of the fourteen pictures drawn and judged in round 7, with the passage in the
literature behind each one, so the ones that only failed for lack of data can be picked up
again once the coach has drawn more out of the person. No picks were made after this round;
the recommendation on the table is to build the top concept into the real app on a sandbox.

## The picture spot and what it exchanges with the chat

The picture spot is a fixed region at the top of the chat screen, 390 pixels wide and 132
pixels tall on a phone, one look at rest and a second look after a tap.

It exchanges the same data the chat itself works from: events (their kind, date, date
certainty, the person, spouse or child they are about, a description, a location, and the
four SARF variables), people, pair bonds, and clusters (a name, a reason, and the events in
it). A chip in the coach's words — for an event, a cluster, or a person — lights that thing
on the picture. A tap on the picture can put a chip of its own into the box where he types.

Rulings that bind every concept:
- **[Oracle: R-0359]** the picture's question marks are hidden for now.
- **[Oracle: R-0372]** nothing on the picture draws what changed in the record.
- **[Oracle: R-0376]** what a cluster shows: its one key shift, where the trouble sits and
  whether it moved between people, the opening event and what followed it on a tap, and
  never a count.
- **[Oracle: R-0377]** event dots sit at one height, never at another height or angle.
- **[Oracle: R-0378]** the picture spot shows a picture.
- **[Oracle: R-0379]** the family drawing concept waits on the traditional family diagram
  and auto-arrangement; not building either now.
- **[Oracle: R-0380]** a concept must carry a real diagnostic message on his own record, not
  only in the abstract, or it is kept as an idea rather than built.
- **[Oracle: R-0381]** build the short sideways scroll into the real app on a sandbox, rather
  than run another round of mockups.

The record used to draw every frame: 17 people, 39 events, 5 clusters opening in 1994, 2005,
2009, 2013 and 2019, 8 pair bonds, and 3 symptom events, all his own. Each concept also says
what it does at 120 events, a record about three times the size.

## The method rules learned

- **Mockup method, corrected 2026-09-23.** Three-D or motion earns its place only when it
  communicates what a flat picture cannot, and the frame has to say what that is by itself.
  A mockup's tap does what the app's tap does — pick, words on the picture, a chip lights, a
  second tap speaks — built on the app's own picture code, never invented for the mockup.
  Title and citation are the only prose allowed under a concept. If a stranger cannot read
  the frame unaided, the concept fails; the people who use the app never see prose. This
  followed his review of round 7's own frames: one drawing had invented its own tap, a circle
  appearing that lined up with nothing on the picture, instead of the tap the app already has.
- **A picture that needs that much prose does not speak.** What he sees on a gallery page is
  the frames, one sentence per concept, and the decisions in front of him. The gates a
  concept passed, the passages behind it, what it costs, and the checks run on it belong in
  the verdict record, never on the page he looks at.
- **Every gallery passes a visual critique before Patrick sees it.** A separate agent from
  the one that drew the frames reviews every one of them: what message it carries, whether
  every mark in it is explained, whether it is busier than the picture already in the app,
  whether anything vanished between frames without saying why, and whether any word was
  invented. It argues keep or kill per frame, and only the survivors are shown to him.

## The fourteen concepts

Twelve were written up in the round's ideas record. A thirteenth, the gap between a
cluster's opening event and its first symptom, was cut from that record in favor of a
revised twelfth concept but was still drawn and judged on its own, since its passage was
judged worth keeping. A fourteenth, the line already built in the app with a short sideways
scroll, was added to meet Patrick's own request that at least one concept extend what is
already there rather than replace it. The six that survived the critic's judgment are listed
first, ranked; the eight that were killed follow, grouped as the record groups them, each
with the reason it failed.

### Survivors, ranked

**1. The line we have, with a short sideways scroll.**
Passage: "Fairly exact dates of when symptoms developed or recurred are important" — Family
Evaluation (Kerr and Bowen), line 3127.
At a glance: the picture already in the app, parked at today, with a pale line running past
the left edge to show there is more; five soft boxes are the five clusters, sized to the time
each covers, and dots at one height are the events that sit in no cluster.
Tap: tapping a chip in the coach's words, or anything on the line, slides the line sideways
until that thing is on screen, draws it larger, and puts a chip for it in the message box;
the years shown at the bottom change to match, but the line itself and its height stay put.
Chip: a cluster chip or event chip slides the line to it and lights it; a person chip lights
every mark about that person without moving the line.
Gives up: the whole history is no longer in one look — the earliest years are a swipe away,
and while looking at them today is off screen.
Critic's verdict: it is the only picture that keeps every event at its true distance in time
and reads at a glance, and at 120 events a fixed rule holds it to at most two screens.
Patrick's word: the first version of this concept invented its own tap — a circle appearing
that lined up with nothing — instead of the tap the app already has; that correction is now
the method rule above, and the recommendation is to build this one into the real app next.

**2. Only words: one line per cluster.**
Passage: "let the calendar 'speak' when others are able to 'hear'" — Family Therapy in
Clinical Practice (Bowen), line 1170.
At a glance: no drawing at all — five short lines of text, oldest first, each giving the
year, what opened that cluster, and what moved, written by the coach from the cluster's name
and reason.
Tap: tapping a line puts that cluster's chip in the message box, fades the other lines, and
expands the tapped line to its full reason and the names of the people in it, each one its
own person chip.
Chip: a cluster chip underlines its line; an event chip underlines that event's words inside
its line; a person chip underlines their name wherever it appears.
Gives up: any sense of time gaps, or of where the trouble sat between people, beyond what the
words themselves say.
Critic's verdict: a stranger reads five short sentences and knows the history with nothing to
decode, and it is the one concept that does not get worse as the record grows.

**3. The family drawing with the trouble lit, and a strip of clusters to move it.**
Passage: "the symbols 'are a useful shorthand'" — Family Evaluation, line 3255.
At a glance: the family drawing in the app's own style, small enough to fit the whole family
in the picture, with the person carrying the symptoms in the most recent cluster lit warm;
under it, a strip of small dashes, one per cluster, with the latest one filled.
Tap: tapping a dash puts that cluster's chip in the message box and moves the warm light to
whoever carried the symptoms in that cluster; tapping a person puts their chip in the box.
Chip: a person chip lights their symbol; an event chip lights the symbol of the person it is
about and fills the dash of its cluster; a cluster chip fills its dash and moves the light.
Gives up: time is not shown as a line — the order of clusters is only left to right, not to
scale.
Critic's verdict: the only survivor that answers who the family is and where the trouble sits
now, and it does not change at 120 events since it draws people rather than events.
More data would help: the concept as drawn needs the traditional family diagram and
auto-arrangement of that diagram, which the app does not build yet [R-0379]; that is a goal,
not current work.

**4. A stack of generations, one shelf each.**
Passage: "the aim is to follow the total family through time with a focus on related events
in interlocking fields" — Family Therapy in Clinical Practice, line 1170.
At a glance: three shelves — his grandparents' generation on top, his parents' in the middle,
his own and any siblings' at the bottom — with a cluster drawn as a thin vertical thread
tying together its events wherever they sit, so a cluster that reached into an older
generation stands visibly taller than one that did not.
Tap: tapping a thread puts that cluster's chip in the message box and enlarges its events
with their descriptions; tapping a shelf's label puts the chip of whoever's shelf it is.
Chip: a cluster chip lights its thread; an event chip lights its dot on its shelf; a person
chip lights their name on their shelf.
Gives up: fine timing inside one shelf, and any generation beyond three.
Critic's verdict: the only survivor that shows a cluster reaching into an older generation,
which is the multigenerational view the sources keep asking for.
More data would help: on his own record only one of the five clusters actually reaches a
shelf above his own, and the two deaths in the record show no consequence recorded in anyone
else, so the concept has no real diagnostic message yet [R-0380]. It would earn that message
once the coach has drawn out how the older generations' own anxiety, functioning and
relationships shifted after each death, separation and move — the true subject the concept
is meant to carry, the shock wave crossing generations. Kept as an idea, not built.

**5. The gap between the opening event and the symptom, one pair per cluster.**
Passage: a correlation between dates "does not establish a relationship, but is suggestive of
one" — Family Evaluation, line 3127; shifts came "between four to twelve weeks before" —
Havstad and Sheffield, line 43.
At a glance: one filled dot per cluster for its opening event; where that cluster also holds
a symptom event, a second, hollow dot marks it, a thin line joins the pair, and the words
above the line say how far apart they fell, such as about a year.
Tap: tapping a pair puts that cluster's chip in the message box and grows the pair to the
full width with what each event was, in words, underneath.
Chip: a cluster chip lights its pair; an event chip lights its dot when the dot is a first
event or a symptom; a person chip lights every dot about that person.
Gives up: everything except two dates per cluster — no other events, no anxiety, no
functioning, no relationship, nobody named.
Critic's verdict: the most honest picture in the round, and the only one whose own words say
out loud that nearness in time is not proof of anything.
More data would help: on his record three of the five clusters hold no symptom event at all,
so three of the five marks are a lone dot with a year and nothing else to say; it would carry
more once more of his clusters held a symptom event of their own.

**6. A carved strip you rotate: clusters as hills.**
Passage: "the magnitude of the events... are used to determine the level of stress a family
is under" — Family Evaluation, line 3355.
At a glance: a strip of carved ground running left to right in time, drawn live in three
dimensions at a slight tilt; each cluster is a hill whose height comes from the kinds of its
events, not their count, and the flat ground between hills is drawn as long as the quiet
years actually were.
Tap: tapping a hill puts that cluster's chip in the message box and centres the strip on it,
named; dragging turns the strip a little to see the older events carved on its far side.
Chip: a cluster chip lights its hill; an event chip lights a notch on the hill; a person chip
lights every hill in their color.
Gives up: the four variables, and it risks being read as "a taller hill is a worse time,"
which the record's own account of clusters rules out.
Critic's verdict: the only one of the four three-dimensional concepts where a stranger can
say something true without reading a caption — nothing much for a long time, then things came
close together — and that quiet span is a finding the sources name in its own right.
Patrick's word: the rotation itself communicates nothing.

### Killed

**7. People as the rows, trouble as a light that moves between them.**
Passage: "The grandmother rather than the adolescent became a repository of family anxiety" —
Family Evaluation, line 3183.
At a glance: a thin row per person who has a symptom event, with one warm band running along
the picture that sits on whichever person's row holds the symptoms at that time and hops from
row to row as it moves.
Tap: tapping a span of the band puts that cluster's chip in the message box and names the
cluster; tapping a row's name puts that person's chip in the box.
Chip: a person chip lights their whole row; an event chip lights its dot in their row; a
cluster chip lights that span of the band.
Gives up: anxiety, functioning and relationship are not drawn, only where the symptom sat.
Critic's verdict: killed for not carrying its own passage — on his record the symptoms never
move off him, so the picture is one lit row and one unlabeled row of everyone else.
More data would help: it would be the plainest drawing of trouble moving that the round
produced, once the record holds a symptom sitting with two or more different people.

**8. The four variables as one shape per cluster.**
Passage: "organizing shifts in all four variables into a single timeline" — Havstad and
Sheffield, line 58.
At a glance: one four-cornered shape per cluster, a corner for each variable — symptom,
anxiety, functioning, relationship — pulled out toward whichever corner moved in that
cluster.
Tap: tapping a shape puts its cluster's chip in the message box, fades the other shapes, and
grows the tapped one to show the words for each corner that moved.
Chip: a cluster chip lights its shape; an event chip lights the shape's corner for the
variable it moved; a person chip lights every shape holding one of their events.
Gives up: exact dates, the gap between clusters, and the direction a variable moved.
Critic's verdict: killed for not being one sentence a stranger can say — reading a
lopsided shape needs corner labels and a comparison the picture's own rules forbid — and for
asserting, on this record, that anxiety never moved, when in fact one event moves it, in an
event that sits in no cluster at all.

**9. The chart Havstad draws: three thin curves and the ticks under them.**
Passage: "the organization of the events identified in the selected text into a timeline that
put shifts in the variables into chronological order" — Havstad and Sheffield, line 56.
At a glance: three thin stepped curves stacked in the picture — symptom on top, anxiety in
the middle, functioning below — each stepping at the date of the event that moved it, with a
row of dots at one height under them for every other event.
Tap: tapping a step or a dot puts that event's chip in the message box and shows its
description; tapping a tinted cluster band puts the cluster's chip in the box.
Chip: an event chip lights its step or dot; a cluster chip lights its band; a person chip
lights every step and dot about them.
Gives up: whose curve it is — the three curves are the record's, not one person's.
Critic's verdict: killed for not carrying its passage on this record — anxiety only steps
once and the symptom only ever steps up, not the anxiety-and-functioning-first pattern the
picture claims — and for being the busiest picture in the round, where a crowded span of
events would read as a worse time, which the record's own account of clusters rules out.

**10. The opening event and its aftershocks as rings.**
Passage: the emotional shock wave is "a network of underground 'aftershocks'... that can
occur anywhere in the extended family system in the months or years following" — Family
Therapy in Clinical Practice, line 2474.
At a glance: one set of rings per cluster, the opening event at the centre and every later
event a dot on a ring whose distance out is how many months after the opening it came.
Tap: tapping a ring set puts that cluster's chip in the message box, grows the set to fill
the picture, and gives each dot its short description.
Chip: a cluster chip lights its ring set; an event chip lights its dot; a person chip lights
every dot about them.
Gives up: the calendar order between clusters is left to right only, not to scale.
Critic's verdict: killed because a stranger can name no mark at rest — nothing on the picture
says that distance from the centre means time — and drawing later events as ticks at every
angle around the ring dodges rather than keeps the rule that event dots sit at one height.

**11. The family as one sheet that dents where the trouble sits.**
Passage: a family "is in functional equilibrium when it is calm and each member is
functioning at reasonable efficiency," and that balance is "disturbed by either the addition
of a new member or the loss of a member" — Family Therapy in Clinical Practice, line 2470.
At a glance: a soft sheet in three dimensions, each person a small weight resting on it in
their generation's row, the sheet dipping under whoever carries the symptoms; at rest it
plays one slow pass through the years before holding at today.
Tap: tapping a weight pauses the pass and puts that person's chip in the message box; tapping
the sheet while it plays pauses it on the cluster at that point and puts that chip in the box.
Chip: a person chip brightens their weight; an event chip jumps the pass to that date; a
cluster chip plays only that cluster's span.
Gives up: dates are not readable, and the pass has to be watched rather than glanced at.
Critic's verdict: killed for not being a single glance — it runs once through the years over
eight seconds — and on this record the dip forms under him once and never moves elsewhere,
so the one thing the picture exists to show never happens.

**12. The shock wave as a drop on water.**
Passage: the shock wave follows "most often a death or threatened death," and a parent, an
important child, or the "head of the clan" are the ones most likely followed by prolonged
disturbance — Family Therapy in Clinical Practice, line 2474 and 2484.
At a glance: a still water surface in three dimensions, time running from far to near; each
cluster's opening event is a drop already landed, its rings spreading and fading with the
months, with symptoms in other people as small splashes riding those rings.
Tap: tapping a ring set puts that cluster's chip in the message box and slides the surface to
bring it to the front, named; tapping a splash puts that event's chip in the box.
Chip: a cluster chip brightens its rings; an event chip lights its splash or drop; a person
chip lights every splash about them.
Gives up: dates, and the four variables, which only appear as splashes.
Critic's verdict: killed on color — the record's symptom events are drawn in the color ruled
to mean the coach is asking a question, when a symptom is data — and because the two widest
drops on this record are the two deaths, which sit in no cluster, so the biggest thing on the
picture is not one of the five the coach actually talks about.

**13. A strip of family snapshots, one per cluster, that scrolls sideways a screen or two.**
Passage: at each important event, do a "'fix' on the entire family system, including place,
date, ages of each person in the household and the functioning of each" — Family Therapy in
Clinical Practice, line 1158.
At a glance: a strip of small panels, one per cluster, oldest on the left, each panel the
family drawing as it stood partway through that cluster's years, with the person who carried
the symptoms filled in.
Tap: tapping a panel grows it to the full picture, names whoever carried the symptoms, and
lists that cluster's events in one line underneath; tapping a person sends their chip.
Chip: a cluster chip slides the strip to its panel and lights it; an event chip slides to its
cluster's panel and lights the person it is about; a person chip lights their symbol in every
panel without moving the strip.
Gives up: time is not to scale — every panel is the same width whatever the real gap between
clusters was — and the four variables are not drawn beyond who is filled in.
Critic's verdict: killed on density, with more marks on the resting screen than the round's
own limit allows, and because on this record the two whole panels on screen at rest draw the
same family in the same arrangement with nobody filled in either one, so the scroll shows
nothing new until the person swipes.

**14. People on a turning ring, time as the turn.**
Passage: anxiety "shifted from one to another in the family as a function of the automatic
ways the family managed emotional discomfort," and when it shifted, "the patient's symptoms
moderated" — Havstad and Sheffield, line 25.
At a glance: a ring of small person symbols in perspective, turned so that whoever carries
the symptoms now sits at the front, with a thin strip at the bottom marking time and a marker
sitting at today.
Tap: dragging the bottom strip turns the ring to bring whoever carried the symptoms at that
time to the front and names the cluster of that time above; letting go sends that cluster's
chip. Tapping a symbol sends that person's chip.
Chip: a person chip turns the ring to bring them forward; an event chip turns it to that
event's date; a cluster chip turns to the cluster's start.
Gives up: the whole history at once — only one time is visible per turn of the ring.
Critic's verdict: killed because a stranger can name no mark — ten unlabeled shapes on an
oval, with a row of event dots underneath doing the picture's real work — and on this record
the ring never actually turns, since every symptom event is his alone.
